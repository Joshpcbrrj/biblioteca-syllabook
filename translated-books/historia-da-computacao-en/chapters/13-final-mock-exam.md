# 13. Final mock exam

> 🗺️ **In this chapter:** 24 questions on the whole book, with at least two per chapter, so you can see what stuck and what you need to review. · ⏱️ ~60 min

Take it without looking anything up, with pen and paper, and only then open the answers. Each answer has a link to the chapter that explains it. At the end, the diagnosis table tells you what to review.

**1.** Put in order, oldest to newest: Jacquard loom, Salamis tablet, arithmometer, Pascaline.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Salamis tablet (c. 300 BCE), Pascaline (1642), Jacquard loom (1801–1803) and arithmometer (produced from 1820). Chapter 1: [Counting before computing](01-counting-before-computing-from-the-abacus.md).

</details>

**2.** True or false: "ENIAC was, without dispute, the first computer." Justify your answer.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

False. "First computer" depends on the definition: the Z3 (relays, 1941), the ABC (1942), Colossus (vacuum tubes, to attack the Lorenz cipher) and ENIAC (vacuum tubes, programmed with wires and switches) each meet different parts of it. Chapter 5: [War and vacuum tubes](05-war-and-vacuum-tubes-zuse-colossus-and-eniac.md).

</details>

**3.** Which kit computer was on the cover of *Popular Electronics* in January 1975, and how much did the kit cost?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The Altair 8800. The kit cost $297 ($395 with a case). Chapter 9: [A computer on every desk](09-a-computer-on-every-desk.md).

</details>

**4.** What kind of operation did Babbage's Difference Engine perform, and which version was built in 2002?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It calculated tables of polynomials using only additions. No. 1 was left unfinished; No. 2, designed in 1847–1849, was built in 2002. Chapter 2: [Babbage and Lovelace](02-babbage-and-lovelace-the-machine-that-was-never.md).

</details>

**5.** Put in order: AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol, the Transformer, the Dartmouth proposal, ChatGPT, Deep Blue beats Kasparov.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Dartmouth (1955), Deep Blue (1997), AlphaGo (2016), the Transformer (2017) and ChatGPT (2022). Chapter 11: [Machines that learn](11-machines-that-learn-the-long-road-of-artificial.md).

</details>

**6.** Explain in your own words what a Turing machine is and what makes it "universal".

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It is an abstract model of computing, with a tape, a head that reads and writes, and a table of states (1936–37). The universal machine reads the table of another machine and does what it would do. Chapter 4: [The idea of a universal machine](04-the-idea-of-a-universal-machine-boole-turing.md).

</details>

**7.** Put in order: the web proposed by Berners-Lee, iPhone announced, TCP/IP adopted by ARPANET, ARPANET switched on.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

ARPANET (October 1969), TCP/IP (January 1, 1983), the web proposal (1989; released at CERN in 1991) and the iPhone (announced January 9, 2007). Chapter 10: [Everything connected](10-everything-connected-arpanet-the-web.md).

</details>

**8.** A punched card from the 1890 census and a Jacquard loom card both have holes. What does each one hold?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The census card holds data (each hole is an answer); the loom card held instructions (the pattern to weave). Chapter 3: [Cards, censuses and flesh-and-blood computers](03-cards-censuses-and-flesh-and-blood-computers.md).

</details>

**9.** What did the Manchester Baby do on June 21, 1948?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It ran a program stored in its own memory: according to the IEEE plaque, it was the first computer to execute a program stored in addressable electronic memory. The EDSAC did the same on May 6, 1949. Chapter 6: [The program moves into memory](06-the-program-moves-into-memory-von-neumann.md).

</details>

**10.** What does a compiler do, and why did it matter for the birth of software?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It translates a program written in a high-level language (closer to human speech) into instructions the machine executes. That way, programmers no longer had to write everything in machine language. Examples: Hopper's A-0 (1952), FORTRAN (1957), COBOL (1960). Chapter 8: [Teaching the machine to understand people](08-teaching-the-machine-to-understand-people.md).

</details>

**11.** What are the three layers for checking a legend, and what goes in each?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Fact (what the source supports), interpretation (what is concluded from the fact, with some risk) and legend (what is repeated as fact without the source supporting it). Chapter 12: [Final project](12-final-project-your-computing-museum.md).

</details>

**12.** Link the transistor, the integrated circuit and the microprocessor in a chain of cause and effect, with a Brazilian milestone that depended on it.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The transistor (1947) replaced the vacuum tube with a semiconductor switch; the integrated circuit (Kilby 1958, Noyce 1959) put a complete circuit in a single piece of material; the microprocessor (Intel 4004, 1971) put the processor on one chip. The Patinho Feio, from the Poli-USP (1972), is the chapter's Brazilian milestone. Chapter 7: [From the transistor to the chip](07-from-the-transistor-to-the-chip.md).

</details>

**13.** What is the difference between calculating and computing? Use the recipe analogy.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Calculating is doing one sum at a time; computing is following instructions that can be swapped, like the same kitchen preparing different dishes depending on the recipe. Chapter 1: [Counting before computing](01-counting-before-computing-from-the-abacus.md).

</details>

**14.** Are the internet and the web the same thing? Give an example that shows the difference.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

No. The internet is the network that links networks, using TCP/IP; the web is a service of pages with hyperlinks that runs on top of it. The web was proposed by Berners-Lee in 1989, but the network had existed since before that (ARPANET, 1969). Chapter 10: [Everything connected](10-everything-connected-arpanet-the-web.md).

</details>

**15.** Was it a moth that created the word "bug"? What do the sources show?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

No. The 1947 moth existed, but "bug" was already in use before, with records from 1878 (Edison) and 1889. Telling the episode as a curiosity is an acceptable interpretation; saying that the word was born there is legend. Chapter 8: [Teaching the machine to understand people](08-teaching-the-machine-to-understand-people.md).

</details>

**16.** Name two Brazilian milestones from this book. Why does calling one of them "the first in the world" require care?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Examples: the ITA's Zezinho (1961), the Patinho Feio (1972), Law 7,232 (1984) and AlterNex (1989). Claiming "first in the world" without a source is a common mistake: stick to what the sources say, what was done, when and by whom. Chapter 12: [Final project](12-final-project-your-computing-museum.md).

</details>

**17.** What was Colossus for, and why did it take so long to enter history?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It was a vacuum-tube machine, designed by Flowers, to attack the Lorenz cipher. Its use stayed secret until the 1970s. Chapter 5: [War and vacuum tubes](05-war-and-vacuum-tubes-zuse-colossus-and-eniac.md).

</details>

**18.** About Ada Lovelace and the title of "first programmer": what is fact, what is interpretation and what is legend?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Fact: the 1843 Notes and Note G, on Bernoulli numbers. Interpretation: calling her the "first programmer" is a debated title, and the sources disagree. Legend: "she wrote the first computer program and invented programming single-handedly", which the sources do not support: the exact authorship is uncertain and Babbage may have collaborated. Chapter 2: [Babbage and Lovelace](02-babbage-and-lovelace-the-machine-that-was-never.md).

</details>

**19.** What did Law 7,232 of 1984 establish?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The National Informatics Policy: transitory protection for national companies, preference in public purchasing and import control for 8 years. The expression "market reserve" does not appear in the text of the law: it is a name given later to the set of restrictions. Chapter 9: [A computer on every desk](09-a-computer-on-every-desk.md).

</details>

**20.** What did Shannon show about circuits, and what is a bit?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

In his master's thesis at MIT, he showed that switches and relays can be described by Boolean algebra. In the 1948 paper, he used the bit as a unit: a choice between two options (the book does not claim who coined the word). Chapter 4: [The idea of a universal machine](04-the-idea-of-a-universal-machine-boole-turing.md).

</details>

**21.** What is the difference in method between Deep Blue (1997) and AlphaGo (2016)?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Deep Blue won by brute force, evaluating about 200 million positions per second (according to IBM). AlphaGo combined neural networks, which learned from games, and search; it beat Lee Sedol 4 to 1 in March 2016. Chapter 11: [Machines that learn](11-machines-that-learn-the-long-road-of-artificial.md).

</details>

**22.** What do the UNIVAC I and the name IBM have to do with the start of the computer industry?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The UNIVAC I was sold from 1951 (46 units, according to the CHM) and is one of the first computers for sale. IBM adopted that name in 1924, from the C-T-R of 1911, and entered the game with machines like the 701 and the 650. Chapter 6: [The program moves into memory](06-the-program-moves-into-memory-von-neumann.md).

</details>

**23.** Who were the "human computers"? Give a documented example.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

People, men and women, whose job was calculating by hand. At the Langley laboratory (NACA), the "computer pool" team was made up of women, and by 1946 about 400 had been trained. Chapter 3: [Cards, censuses and flesh-and-blood computers](03-cards-censuses-and-flesh-and-blood-computers.md).

</details>

**24.** What does Moore's law say, and what does it *not* say?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It is the observation, from 1965 (revised in 1975), that the number of components per chip doubles at regular intervals, now counted at about two years. It is not a law of nature, and it does not say that computers get "twice as fast" every two years. Chapter 7: [From the transistor to the chip](07-from-the-transistor-to-the-chip.md).

</details>
