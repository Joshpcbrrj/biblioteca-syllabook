# 0. How to study this book

This book tells the history of computing, from gear-driven calculators to generative AI, with dates and names checked against sources, and shows why each turning point happened. You don't need to know how to program: the code snippets are short, optional for reading, and there to test ideas.

## How the book is organized

- There are 12 chapters in four parts (chapters 1 to 3, 4 to 6, 7 to 9 and 10 to 12). Each part opens with a summary sheet.
- Every chapter has the same rhythm: warm-up, explanation with examples, **Practice**, **Quick review**, an AI tutor and the summary.
- Sections that start with 🔎 are **Going deeper**: skip them without guilt if you are in a hurry.
- Boxes that start with ⚖️ separate **fact** (what the sources record) from **interpretation** (what is concluded from them, and may vary). Don't skip them: they help you read any history text more carefully.
- Answers are hidden under **See answer**. In the EPUB, they move to an appendix, in chapter order.
- Chapters 6 to 10 and 12 bring Brazilian milestones. They tell the same story, seen from Brazil.
- At the end there is a **Final mock exam** and a **Glossary**.

## Four study techniques

1. **Answer before looking.** Try each exercise before opening the answer, even if you get it wrong: the effort is what makes it stick.
2. **Come back on the suggested days.** The 🔁 *Review today* block at the end of each chapter says which chapters to reread.
3. **Explain it in your own words.** Tell a friend, or say out loud, what you just read. If you get stuck, reread.
4. **Mix old and new.** Redo exercises from earlier chapters every now and then.

## Study paces

The book adds up to about 585 minutes of study across the 12 chapters. Depending on how much time you have per day:

| Pace | Per day | Finish in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light | ~20 min | ~30 days (4 weeks) |
| Medium | ~40 min | ~15 days (2 weeks) |
| Intense | ~90 min | ~7 days (1 week) |

If you skip days, just add them to the deadline. Don't rush: one chapter per session, at most.

## How to use the reviews, the AI tutor and the mock exam

- **Quick review:** five short questions with hidden answers. Do it the next day, without rereading the chapter.
- **Study with an AI tutor:** ready-made prompts to paste into an assistant. Ask it to question you one thing at a time and not to give the answer before you try. Check important facts against the chapter's sources: an AI can get dates wrong.
- **Final mock exam:** 24 questions on the whole book. Take it only after chapter 12, without looking anything up, and use the diagnosis table at the end to see what to review.

## 🎯 Placement test

Already know part of the subject? Answer without looking anything up, one question at a time, and check the answers. If you know nothing, you can start right at chapter 1.

**1.** What was the difference between *calculating* and *computing*, and what does the Jacquard loom have to do with it?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

Calculating is doing one sum at a time; computing is following instructions that can be swapped. On the Jacquard loom, swapping the punched card swapped the pattern woven: the card was the instruction. Chapter 1.

</details>

**2.** Was Babbage's Analytical Engine ever completed? Which of its parts resemble a computer today?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

No, it was never completed. It had a memory (the Store), a processor (the Mill) and used cards to guide the operations. Chapter 2.

</details>

**3.** Why did the 1890 US census lead to Hollerith's machine?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

There were about 63 million people, and the estimate was that the manual method would not finish in time. Hollerith won the contest with punched cards and a tabulating machine. Chapter 3.

</details>

**4.** What is a bit?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The unit of information: a choice between two options (0 or 1, yes or no). Shannon used it as a unit in the 1948 paper; the book does not claim who coined the word. Chapter 4.

</details>

**5.** Who built the first computer? Why does this question have no single answer?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It depends on the definition of "computer": the Z3 (relays, 1941), the ABC (1942), Colossus (vacuum tubes, to break ciphers) and ENIAC (vacuum tubes) each meet different parts of it. Chapter 5.

</details>

**6.** What does "stored program" mean, and why did it allow the computer industry?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The program sits in the same memory as the data, so switching tasks means changing the contents of memory, without rewiring. This made it possible to sell the same machine for different uses. Chapter 6.

</details>

**7.** Which came first: the transistor or the integrated circuit? And what does Moore's law say?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The transistor (December 1947); the integrated circuit came later (Kilby in 1958, Noyce in 1959). Moore's law is the observation, from 1965 and revised in 1975, that the number of components per chip doubles about every two years. Chapter 7.

</details>

**8.** Did the word "bug" come from a moth in 1947? Justify your answer.

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

No. The 1947 moth existed, but the word already had earlier records, such as in 1878 (Edison) and in 1889. Chapter 8.

</details>

**9.** What is the difference between the internet and the web?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

The internet is the network that links networks (TCP/IP); the web is a service of pages with hyperlinks that runs on top of it, proposed by Berners-Lee in 1989. Chapter 10.

</details>

**10.** What did Deep Blue do against Kasparov in 1997, and why is that not "learning"?

<details>
<summary>See answer</summary>

It won by brute force, evaluating about 200 million positions per second ("brute force", in IBM's words). It did not learn to play the way the neural networks that came later did. Chapter 11.

</details>
