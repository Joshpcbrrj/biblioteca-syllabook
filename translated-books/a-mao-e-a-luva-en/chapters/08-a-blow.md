# 8. A Blow

One morning Estêvão woke with his resolution made to strike the decisive blow. Weak hearts have such sudden energies, and it is the nature of pusillanimity to deceive itself. He confessed that he had done nothing, and that the situation demanded something more.

"Circumstances were never more propitious than today," the young man thought; "Guiomar treats me with an affability of good omen. Besides, there is a lofty spirit in her; she will recognize that a discreet and respectful feeling, like this of mine, is worth a little more than drawing-room flatteries."

The resolution was settled; what remained was the means of carrying it out. Estêvão hesitated a long time between saying aloud what he felt and conveying it on paper. Either way had for him more dangers than advantages. He feared being cold in a written declaration or incomplete in an oral confession. Irresolute and wavering, he adopted both means and rejected them, at short intervals; at last he put off the choice to another occasion.

Chance supplied what resolution lacked, and the premeditated gave way to the fortuitous.

One afternoon, some people being at dinner in the baroness's house, they went out to walk in the grounds. Estêvão, who, like Luís Alves, was among the guests, gradually drew away from the other groups and approached that historic fence where, after two years of absence and forgetting, he had seen the lovely Guiomar, now transformed. It was the first time he had set eyes on that spot since the conversation he had had with her there. The emotion he felt was naturally great; the picture rose again before his eyes, the hour, the bright sky, the sweet breath of morning, and finally the figure of the girl, who had appeared there like the soul of the picture, bringing him memories he had thought dead, hopes he had supposed impossible.

Estêvão bowed his head under the sweet weight of those memories, his soul drank in long draughts all the life that his imagination created for him, and perhaps night would have found him in the same attitude, had not Guiomar's melodious voice said to him, a few paces away:

"Doctor, have you lost something?"

The young man turned his head quickly, and saw the girl, who was crossing one of the nearby paths, looking and smiling at him. Estêvão smiled too, and with a presence of mind quite rare in lovers, especially in lovers such as he was, promptly answered:

"I have lost nothing, but I have found something."

"Let us see what it was."

And Guiomar drew near, with a firm and steady step, and Estêvão, without much wavering, forged on the spot a philosophical reflection about an insect that happened to be passing over a dry leaf. The reflection was not worth much, and had the defect of coming somewhat forced and dragged in; the girl smiled, however, and was about to continue on her way, when he, gathering all his strength, made her stop with these words:

"And if I had found something else?"

"Better still!" she exclaimed, turning back with a laugh.

Estêvão took two steps toward Guiomar, this time moved and resolute. The girl grew serious and prepared to hear him.

"If I had found in this place," he went on, "long days of hope and longing, a past I had thought would never live again, a hidden and fearful pain, lived in solitude, nourished and consoled by my own tears? If I had found here the torn page of a story begun and interrupted, not by anyone's fault on earth, but by the sinister star of my life, which an evil angel lit in Heaven, and which perhaps, perhaps no one will ever put out?"

Estêvão fell silent and stood looking fixedly at Guiomar.

That sudden declaration, face to face, was so far from the young man's temperament that she spent some long seconds before she came back from her astonishment. He himself marveled at the audacity he had shown; and as he hung upon the girl's lips, he went over in his memory, though confusedly, what had so feebly come from his breast in that hour of blessed temerity.

"If you had found all that," answered Guiomar, smiling, "it is natural that you would have preferred to find something less melancholy. Yet it seems you found nothing more than this occasion to speak, with the lively imagination God gave you; in either case, however, I can certainly pity you or admire you, but it is not given to me to hear you."

And Guiomar was about to move away again, when Estêvão, fearing to lose the occasion that fortune offered him, said from a distance in a sad and suppliant voice:

"Hear me for a single minute!"

"Not one, but ten," answered the girl, stopping short and turning her face toward him, "and they will probably be the last in which we speak alone. I yield to the compassion your state inspires in me; and since you have broken the long and expressive silence in which you have kept yourself until today, I grant that you may say everything, in order to hear a single word from me."

The girl had spoken in a dry and imperious tone, in which impatience dominated more than the compassion she had alluded to. Estêvão's heart beat as never before, as the heart is wont to beat in the crises of supreme anguish. All that castle of wind, laboriously built in his days of illusion, crumbled and came undone, wind that it was. Estêvão repented of the impulse that had led him to violate once more the secret of his inmost feelings, to give up so many hopes, nourished with the best of his youthful blood.

Some moments passed in which neither spoke; they seemed to be measuring each other, she serene and quiet, he trembling and frozen.

"A single word," repeated Estêvão, "and I guess it will be one of disenchantment. No matter! Since I have dared to say something to you, I must tell you everything, happy if there remains to me at least the greatest fortune I can now aspire to: your remorse."

Guiomar had listened to him calmly; the last word made her start. She smiled, however, with a somewhat willed smile, and waited.

The narration was long, as long as the occasion, the place, and the person allowed; it lasted barely ten minutes. Estêvão hid nothing from her, neither the love he had once had for her, nor the one that was now reborn in him, more violent than the first; he told her of the sufferings he had endured, the hopes that at last blossomed in his soul, everything he had undertaken to have the happiness of contemplating her from close by, of enjoying in that scant spot of earth the greatest of all blessedness.

Such is the transcription, not literal, but faithful, of what Estêvão said during those ten minutes. The words fell from him trembling and his voice came out subdued, partly because he strove to stifle it so that no one would hear, partly because emotion tightened his throat. The pain was visibly sincere; the eloquence came from the heart.

Guiomar had not heard it all with the same expression; at first a half smile seemed to open on her lips, but before long a more compassionate and human veil fell down her face. There was in her impatience and anxiety to finish, to get away from there; it was, without doubt, the fear that the absence might be prolonged in such a way as to inspire suspicion. But there was also compassion and pity.

"No blame can fall on you for the evil I have suffered," said Estêvão, concluding; "least of all now: I alone, my head alone, is the sole cause of everything. I seemed to see the contrary of what existed; I even came to suppose that there was in your heart something that was not total indifference; I see that it was all illusion."

The tone in which he had spoken was the same as the words set down here, all humble and resigned, without the least tinge of complaint or reproach. A submission like that was bound to move a woman who was loved. Guiomar spoke to him without bitterness:

"It was illusion," she said. "The feeling that you have revealed to me whole, no one receives or nourishes at will; nature infuses it or denies it. Can I be to blame for that?"

"None."

"Nor you either, and I hope this mutual justice will strengthen the esteem we ought to have for each other. But esteem only, there can be nothing else, on my part at least. It is little, certainly..."

"It is not little, it is something different," interrupted Estêvão.

"But expect nothing more," concluded Guiomar, without heeding the interruption.

Estêvão opened his mouth to speak, but found no word to tell her what he felt; he put his hand to his heart, which was beating violently, and stood looking at her with dry and fixed eyes, his voice extinguished, as if his soul had wholly fled. It was clear, after that disenchantment, that it behooved him not to return there again, at least with the assiduity of hope; and so even the one bitter satisfaction of seeing her was now no longer to be allowed him.

"I give you a piece of advice," said Guiomar after some seconds of pause: "be a man, conquer yourself; your great defect is to have kept a child's soul."

"Perhaps," answered the young man, sighing.

"And farewell. We have spoken alone more than was fitting; I do not know whether another would consent to this. But I not only acknowledge your respectful feelings, I also wish that these few words now exchanged may put an end to impossible aspirations."

Guiomar held out her hand to him, which he touched lightly.

The baroness appeared, meanwhile, a few fathoms away; she came leaning on her nephew's arm, who was talking to her, but to whom she no longer listened. She had her eyes fixed on the two speakers of a moment before. The girl, as soon as she saw her godmother from afar, boldly gave her arm to Estêvão, and they both went on to meet her; Guiomar's face revealed nothing; Estêvão's came troubled and downcast. The baroness frowned:

"Jorge," she said in a low voice, "we need to talk."
