# 14. Ex-abrupto

The reader will already have understood that the journey to Cantagalo was almost entirely Guiomar's doing. The baroness had at first been reluctant, as she had been on other occasions, and the commander had little hope left of seeing her on his plantation. But Guiomar's vote was decisive. She strengthened the commander's reasons with her own, pleading not only her godmother's obligation to keep her word, but also the advantage those three months of country life, far from the agitations of the Court, might bring her; finally, she invoked her own desire to see a plantation and to learn the habits of the interior.

There was no such desire, nor anything resembling it; but Guiomar knew that in the scales of her godmother's resolutions the satisfaction of a wish of hers weighed heavily. The sacrifice would last three or four months; she would have braved ten or twelve, if so many were needed, to escape for a time Jorge's pretensions, notwithstanding her loathing for any life that was not the sumptuous and bustling life of the Court. I, who am the Plutarch of this illustrious lady, shall not fail to note that in this moment there was in her a little of Alcibiades, that dandy and delightful statesman, to whom spite also gave strength one day to endure Spartan frugality.

Unfortunately, Jorge reduced all these calculations to nothing. She had counted on his excessive attachment to the delights of the Court; she had not counted on the suggestions of Mrs. Oswald, who had perceived the plan and had twisted Jorge's first resolution, which was to stay and wait. His sacrifice was compensated by the probability of victory, which consisted not only in getting for a wife a beautiful and beloved girl, but also in making much more summary the division of what the baroness would leave at her death to the two of them. This consideration, which was not the principal one, still had its weight in Jorge's mind, and, let us be just, it ought to have: to possess was his only trade. So it was that the girl not only failed to obtain a good, but fell from one evil into a greater; to have him at her side, where distractions would be less ready and varied, was equivalent to falling sick of boredom and dying of inanition.

Imagine, then, the state of her mind after Jorge's declaration. There was no way of escaping the suitor; she had to swallow him. This prospect wholly cast down her spirits. A confidante, in such situations, is a gift from heaven; but Guiomar had none, and if some person deserved such confidence from her, it is certain or almost certain that she would tell her nothing. Her sorrows were proud, the sadnesses of her heart had modesty. Spirits of this caste are ignorant of the consolation there is, in hours of crisis, in sharing oneself with another; a sad but happy ignorance, which often spares them contact with a treacherous and wicked conscience.

In the midst of her long reflection, Luís Alves's words sounded in her memory; she heard them again, just as he had uttered them, from the discourteous phrase to the respectful expression. One was the commentary on the other, and both might explain Luís Alves's character to her, if she had a few more elements for knowing him; in any case, it was the corner of the veil lifted. Although one could not read to the bottom of his spirit, one already saw what his method of action was.

Any other man, after the effect produced by the first declaration, would not have dared, or would not have cared, to attempt anything more to undo the plan of the journey. But Luís Alves's mind had the obstinacy of the bulldog. It was necessary to him that the baroness's family should not leave the Court; this object he was bound to attain at all costs. He watched for occasions, took advantage of circumstances, had the skill to insert his request into any scrap of conversation, where it would have seemed least appropriate to anyone else. Jorge applauded him with all the force that his interest could command. The baroness opposed to the lawyer's suggestions the soft and hobbled resistance of one who wants the very thing she refuses.

"The doctor is terrible," she would say. "Once he gets something into his head, no one can get it out again."

"Precisely, it is a fixed idea. Without a fixed idea nothing good is done in this world."

Guiomar upheld her godmother's resolution, though not often, nor in the same dry and imperious tone as on the first night. Her impulse was to be consistent; at the same time she did not wish to appear to Luís Alves's eyes to accept his help in obtaining what she otherwise desired with all her heart; that would be to absolve him of his first fault.

The argument that most influenced everyone's mind, the one that ought to have driven off the idea of such a journey, was the danger of braving the cholera morbus, which at that time was going through some points of the interior. One morning it became known that in Cantagalo the terrible enemy had appeared. This time Luís Alves triumphed without saying a word; the baroness drew back before that brutal fact.

The journey was therefore abandoned, to everyone's satisfaction, except perhaps Mrs. Oswald's, who feared much from the marriageable youth of the Court, and from Guiomar's beautiful chestnut eyes. Mrs. Oswald feared to see a new enemy rise at every step, ambushed in some theater or ball, or at the least in the Rua do Ouvidor, and did not see that the new enemy might very well be literally at the door. The Englishwoman's sagacity was this time somewhat myopic. The reason is that Luís Alves, in all those preliminaries of his, had conducted himself with skill; far from seeking the girl, he seemed to have altered nothing in his feelings, nor to wish to change the kind of relations he had hitherto maintained. Guiomar, meanwhile, could not help comparing that sort of attentive indifference he showed her with the words she had formerly heard from him, and the result of the comparison did not seem to her very clear.

On the night of the same day on which it was settled to put off the journey to better times, some outside people were at the baroness's house; Guiomar, seated at the piano, had just finished playing, at her godmother's request, a passage from a fashionable opera.

"Thank you very much," she said to Luís Alves, who had approached to pay her a compliment. "You are cheerful! It seems to be the satisfaction of having thwarted the greatest wish I had on this occasion."

"It was not I," he said; "it was the epidemic."

"Your ally, it seems."

"Everything is the ally of the man who knows how to will," answered the lawyer, giving this somewhat emphatic phrase the greatest tone of simplicity that could come from his lips.

Guiomar bowed her head and for some moments ran her fingers over the keys, while Luís Alves, taking another piece of music from the top of the piano, said to her:

"You could give us this bit of Bellini, if you wished."

Guiomar took the music mechanically and opened it on the stand.

"So it was your will?" she asked, continuing the interrupted subject of the dialogue.

"Will certainly, because it was necessity."

"Necessity," she returned, beginning to play, less to play than to cover her voice; "but necessity why?"

"For a very simple reason, because I love you."

The music stopped. Guiomar had risen with a start. But neither the girl's gesture nor the surprise of the other people disturbed the lawyer; Luís Alves bent toward the stool, as if to adjust it, and turning to Guiomar said graciously:

"You may sit down now; it is secure."

Guiomar sat down again, mute, spiteful, her heart beating as it had never beaten on any other occasion of her life, neither from fright, nor from anger, nor... from love, I was about to say, though she had never felt it. She did not stay there long; with a trembling hand she leafed through the music that lay open on the stand, soon let it go and rose.

Nobody noticed these last movements; and if anyone had been able to notice anything, the girl had taken it to heart to dispel all suspicions. The first impression had been profound, but Guiomar had strength enough to master herself and shut every feeling up in her heart.

What passed afterwards, when, free from strange eyes, she could give herself up to herself, no one knew, except the mute walls of her room, or the moonbeam filtered through the rare fabric of the window curtains, as if spying on that soul hungry for light. Her mirror, perhaps, knew, when the next day it reflected her undone face and broken eyes. Whether it was the night's meditation that softened and dulled them, the mirror did not ask, naturally because it knew; but perhaps it remarked to itself that if they were more beautiful so, they called for another face on which to fall better. Guiomar's face wanted them as they were, severe, firm and brilliant.

The baroness too could not fail to see that her goddaughter had not awakened with her usual air; she found her taciturn and distracted.

"I, godmother?" asked Guiomar, simulating a smile of surprise.

"It must be a mistake of my eyes."

"It is nothing else; I am as always, as yesterday, as tomorrow. I passed the night a little poorly, it is true; but what I had has entirely disappeared. The proof..."

Guiomar stopped at this point, went over to her godmother and gave her a kiss.

"The proof," she continued, "is that you still find me pretty today, do you not?"

"Child!" answered the baroness, giving her a little pat on the cheek.

The girl's tranquillity was simulated; as soon as her godmother turned her back, the same veil covered her face. She had learned from childhood to disguise her preoccupations.

As for Luís Alves, although he had counted on his crude and abrupt method, he left there without full certainty of the result. This uncertainty shook him more than he supposed; and it was, without doubt, the first occasion on which he felt that he truly loved her, though his love was like himself: placid and master of itself. The next day, Estêvão questioned him about Guiomar.

"I believe," he said, after reflecting some moments, "I believe that for the present you should not lose all hope."
