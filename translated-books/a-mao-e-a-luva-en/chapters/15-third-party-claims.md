# 15. Third-Party Claims

For three days Luís Alves stayed away from the baroness's house, though he was dying to go. But this absence was part of the plan; it was among the instructions he had himself given his heart; there was no remedy but to observe them.

On the fourth day he received a note from the baroness congratulating him on his election. The mail from the North had arrived, and with it the news of the electoral victory. Luís Alves was a deputy; he was at last to lend a hand to the making of the laws. Estêvão was the first to congratulate him; he was the old companion of the academy benches, and more than the others he was bound to applaud that good fortune. He did not hide from him, however, the envy it aroused in him:

"A deputy!" he sighed. "Oh! I might have been a deputy too."

Estêvão said this as a child desires the trinket he sees in another child's lap, nothing more. They were his dreams of former days, which were reborn just as they had been, inconsistent, vague, ready to dissipate at the first ray of morning.

Luís Alves hastened to go and thank the baroness for her congratulations. Guiomar gave a slight start when she saw him, but received him calmly and smilingly, almost indifferent. The lawyer was skillful; he did not pursue her with his eyes; besides rousing the attention of the others, that would be following the common method. He did not wish to resemble the rest.

Guiomar, meanwhile, observed him at intervals, sidelong, as if wishing to catch him unawares; little by little, however, her gaze grew more direct and steady. Luís Alves's was natural and even, as it had been before, as it still was with everyone.

On leaving, by the door of a room where he happened to meet her, Luís Alves had occasion to say to her this simple word:

"Have you forgiven me?"

The girl withdrew her hand, which he held in his, and turned her body away, her eyelids falling at the same time.

"Have you forgiven me?" he repeated.

Guiomar withdrew without a word. Luís Alves waited until she had vanished and left. The girl, meanwhile, was irritated at having answered him nothing, though it was true that she had found nothing, and perhaps would find nothing, to answer; but she repented and thought about it a long time.

Did that mean she loved him? It meant she was on the point of it. The dawn whitened the sky, would then tint the tops of the mountains, would pour at last down the slope, until the sun appeared, the sun contemporary with Adam and with the last man who is to come.

A few days later, Luís Alves, entering the baroness's house, had the good fortune to find the girl alone in the sewing room, from which the baroness had been absent for five minutes. Mrs. Oswald was out. It was the hour of evening; the day was about to drown in the bosom of night.

Guiomar, softly seated on a low chair, had a book open on her knees and her eyes on the air. Luís Alves surprised her in that meditative attitude, lovelier than ever, because so, and at that hour, and with the half-dark dress that set off the milk color of her face, she had something at once graceful and severe that seemed chosen on purpose to receive him.

"My godmother is coming," said Guiomar, soon after extending her hand to him, which he pressed and felt a little trembling.

"In five minutes perhaps," he said; "that is enough to decide my destiny. Twice I have asked you whether you forgave me; for the third time I beg you to answer me; a single word costs little; a single gesture costs still less."

The girl looked for some time at the book before her. But the morning was already high in Guiomar's heart, the light intense, the sun warm and bright, for she did not look long at the book, nor hesitate more than was natural and to be expected on that occasion. Two minutes later she made the gesture, a single gesture, but more eloquent than if she had spoken: she held out her hand to him.

Luís Alves clasped it between his own.

The emotion was natural in both; there they remained some moments silent, he with his eyes fixed on her, she with hers on the floor. Their hands touched and their hearts beat in unison.

Five brief minutes passed so. She was the first to break the silence.

"A gesture, a single gesture, and it is my destiny that I give you with it," said Guiomar, looking squarely at the young man.

"Not yet. If our destinies are joined, I am convinced that my love, at least, will have the virtue of making you happy. But nothing is done yet, and if I was brief and hasty in the confession, I do not wish to be so in the consecration I ask of you."

Luís Alves had fallen silent; the girl looked at him as if seeking to understand him.

"Yes," he went on; "it is better that you should not yield to a moment of enthusiasm. My life is yours; my whole destiny is in your hands... Yet I do not wish to take your heart by surprise at this moment; on the day when you judge me truly worthy of being your husband, I shall hear you and follow you."

The girl's answer was to press his hands, smile, and steep her eyes in his. The baroness's step interrupted this contemplation.

Guiomar loved in earnest. But to what point was that feeling involuntary? It was so to the point of not tarnishing in our heroine the chastity of the heart, of not diminishing in her the force of her affective faculties. Thus far only; from there on, the cold choice of the mind came in. I do not wish to present her as a soul that passion maddens and blinds, nor to make her die of a silent and timid love. She was nothing of the kind, nor would she do so. Her nature demanded and loved those flowers of the heart, but one was not to expect that she would go to pick them in wild and bare places, or from the branches of the modest shrub planted before a rustic window. She wanted them beautiful and lush, but in a Sèvres vase, set upon a rare piece of furniture, between two city windows, the said vase and the said flowers flanked by cashmere curtains, whose ends were to trail on the carpet of the floor.

Could Luís Alves give her this kind of love? He could; she felt that he could. The two ambitions had divined each other from the time that intimacy brought them together.

Luís Alves's conduct, sober, direct, resolute, without faltering or idle excess, made the girl perceive that he was born to conquer, and that his ambition truly had wings, at the same time that his heart had them, or seemed to. Besides, the first step of the public man was taken; he was about to enter fully upon the road that leads the strong to glory. Around him that light was about to be made which was the girl's ambition, the atmosphere she longed to breathe. Estêvão had given her the sentimental life, Jorge the vegetative life; in Luís Alves she saw combined the domestic affections with the noise of the outside world.

Once they understand each other, it is hard for two hearts to hide themselves, at least from the shrewdest eyes. Mrs. Oswald's were among the keenest. The Englishwoman perceived within a short time that there was something between them. To question the girl was useless, and besides dangerous; it would be to go, with a light heart, in search of hatred, perhaps. Yet what if it were still possible to save everything? Guiomar would hardly resist a wish of her godmother; it was possible to win her over on that side.

Mrs. Oswald then conceived an insane project, which seemed to her, however, excellent and well advised. The desire to serve the baroness and to carry an idea through to the end blinded the eyes of her reason.

She went directly to Jorge.

"Do you know what I am thinking?" she said. "I think there is a Moor on the coast."

"A Moor on the coast!" exclaimed Jorge, with such an expression of displeasure that it was easy to understand the foundation of suspicion already existing in his mind.

"Nothing less," said the Englishwoman; "but a Moor that can be captured."

And the Englishwoman set out a complete plan, which the baroness's nephew heard somewhat perplexed. The plan consisted in Jorge's going to ask the baroness for the girl's hand, in the baroness's own presence.

The baroness, who cherished the wish of seeing them married, would not fail to throw her vote into the scales, and it would be very difficult for Guiomar's gratitude not to decide in Jorge's favor.

"Gratitude... and interest," she went on. "We must count also on interest, which is a great private counselor. She will not wish to sacrifice her godmother's affection, which to her is worth..."

"Oh! what a sad thought!" Jorge interrupted, recoiling before Mrs. Oswald's idea.

The Englishwoman smiled and let that argument drop; but she held firm to that of affection. Guiomar would not oppose a wish of her godmother; it was urgent to strike the blow. Jorge did not dare to surprise the girl's acquiescence by that means; but he believed in its efficacy, and above all he feared losing the cause. Once he won it, he could trust everything to time and to his love.

The counsel was followed to the letter. In the evening, in the baroness's presence, at the hour of parting, because he had hesitated most of the time, Jorge performed that insane act of declaring to the girl that he loved her and asking for her hand. His aunt smiled with contentment, but had the prudence to utter nothing while Guiomar, growing pale, said nothing, because she found nothing to say.

The silence lasted some three or four minutes, an awkward and vexed silence, in which neither of them dared to resume the conversation. The baroness, for her part, imagined that the two had at last come to an understanding, and that the declaration was authorized by the girl.

Guiomar's embarrassment was not of a kind to give room for this supposition; but the good lady saw with the eyes of her good wishes.

"For my part," the baroness declared at last, "I do not oppose it; I should be very glad if it ended there. But it is a matter of the heart; I must await Guiomar's answer."

And turning to her goddaughter:

"Think and decide, my child," she said; "and if you are happy, I shall be so even more than you."

Twice the refusal hovered on the girl's lips; but her tongue did not dare to repeat the word of the heart. After some moments:

"I shall think it over," she answered, kissing her godmother's hand; and she went on, turning to Jorge: "Good night! Until tomorrow."
