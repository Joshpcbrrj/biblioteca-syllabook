# 4. Latet anguis

The baroness's walk lasted little more than half an hour. The sun was beginning to warm, and although the grounds were well shaded, the heat advised the good lady to go in. Guiomar gave her her arm, and the two, following the same path, made their way to the house.

"It seems very late, Guiomar," said the baroness after a few seconds.

"And it is, godmother. I stayed out longer today than I usually do, because of a meeting I had here in the grounds."

"A meeting?"

"A man."

"Some thief?" asked her godmother, stopping.

"No, ma'am," answered Guiomar, smiling, "he was not a thief. My teacher from school... do you know she has died?"

"Who said so?"

"Her nephew, the very man I met here today."

"You are joking with me! A man in the grounds?"

"Not exactly in the grounds, but in Dr. Luís Alves's garden. He was leaning on the fence; we exchanged a few words."

The baroness looked at her for a few seconds.

"But, child, that is not pretty. What would they say if they saw you?... I would say nothing, because I know what you are worth, and I know the discretion God gave you. But appearances... What sort of man is this nephew?"

They were interrupted by a woman of forty-four or forty-five, tall and thin, with hair between fair and white, blue eyes, neatly dressed: Senhora Oswald, or, more Britishly, Mrs. Oswald, the baroness's companion for some years. Mrs. Oswald had met the baroness in 1846; a widow with no family, she had accepted the proposals the latter made her. She was an intelligent and shrewd woman, endowed with a good nature and ready to oblige. Before Guiomar went to live with her godmother, Mrs. Oswald had been the soul of the house; the presence of Guiomar, whom the baroness loved extremely, altered the situation a little.

"It is nine o'clock!" said the Englishwoman from a distance. "I thought you did not wish to come home today. The heat is strong; and the baroness knows it is not wise to expose herself to the ardors of the sun, especially in these times of epidemics."

"You are right, Mrs. Oswald; but Guiomar was so late today in coming to fetch me that the walk began late."

"Why did you not send for me?"

"She was perhaps asleep, or absorbed in her Walter Scott..."

"Milton," the Englishwoman corrected gravely. "This morning was devoted to Milton. What an immense poet, Dona Guiomar!"

"As immense as this heat," observed Guiomar, smiling. "Let us quicken our step, and inside we will hear you in a better disposition."

The three went on, climbed the stairs and entered the dining room, which was vast, with six windows onto the grounds. From there they went to a little sitting room, where the baroness sat in her armchair to await the hour of breakfast. Guiomar went out to attend to her toilette; and the baroness, who for some minutes had been head bowed and pensive, looked fixedly at Mrs. Oswald without saying a word.

The baroness was a lady of fifty, plump, dressed with that neatness and care of old age which is a remnant of the elegance of youth. Her hair, the color of dull silver, framed her serene face, somewhat wrinkled, not by sorrows, of which she had had none, but by the years. Her eyes shone with abundant life, and were the most youthful part of her face.

Having married early, she had the good fortune to be equally happy from the day of her betrothal to that of her widowhood. Widowhood had cost her dear; but some years had gone by, and from the raw pain she had suffered there remained to her now the consolation of remembrance.

"Come closer; I need to speak to you alone," she said to the Englishwoman, who was a few steps away.

Mrs. Oswald went to the door to see whether anyone was coming and returned to sit beside the baroness. The baroness was pensive again, with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on the floor.

The two remained there in silence some two or three minutes. The baroness at last awoke from her reflections and turned to the Englishwoman:

"Mrs. Oswald," she said, "it seems to be written that I shall not be completely happy. No dream of mine ever failed me; this one, however, will be no more than a dream, and it was the loveliest of my old age."

"But why do you despair?" said the Englishwoman. "Take heart, and all will be arranged. For my part, would that I could contribute to the complete happiness of this family, to whom I owe so many and such great benefits."

"Benefits!"

"And what else are your kindnesses, the protection you have given me, the confidence..."

"Enough, enough," the baroness interrupted affectionately; "let us speak of something else."

"Of her, is it not? My heart tells me that with some patience everything will be achieved. All means must be tried; and all of them are good if it is a matter of making your happiness and hers. All's well that ends well, said a poet of ours, a man of sense. For the moment I see only one obstacle: her little inclination..."

"Only that?"

"What other is there?"

"Perhaps another," said the baroness, lowering her voice; "perhaps not, but I am so unlucky in this wish of mine that it may well become an obstacle."

"But what is it?"

"A man, a young man, I do not know who, nephew of the one who was Guiomar's teacher... She herself told me everything a little while ago."

"Everything what?"

"I do not know if everything; but at any rate she told me that while walking in the grounds she had seen this nephew of her teacher, by the fence of Dr. Luís Alves, and stayed to talk with him. What can this be, Mrs. Oswald? Some love that continues or begins again now, now that she is no longer the mere heiress to her parents' poverty, but my daughter, the daughter of my heart."

The baroness's emotion in saying these words was such that Mrs. Oswald took her hands affectionately and sought to comfort her with other words of hope and confidence. She told her, besides, that the mere talking with this man, whom neither of them knew in any case, was no reason to suppose an earlier passion.

"In short," concluded the Englishwoman, "I find it hard to believe that she loves anyone in this world. For the moment I am of the opinion that she likes no one, and our advantage is nothing other than that. Your goddaughter has a singular soul; she passes easily from enthusiasm to coldness, and from confidence to reserve. She will come to love, but I do not think she will have great passions, at least lasting ones. In any case, I can vouch at present for her heart, as if I had the key in my pocket."

The baroness shook her head.

"As for this man," Mrs. Oswald went on, "we shall find out who he is, and what ties of affection there were in the past."

"Do you think it possible?"

"Naturally!"

The Englishwoman uttered this single word with the assurance needed to calm the good lady's mind, who remained a while gazing at her in amazement, like one who was reflecting.

"There are occasions," the baroness said at last, after some seconds of silence, "there are occasions when I almost feel remorse at the love I bear Guiomar. She came to fill in my life the void left by poor Henriqueta, the daughter of my womb, whom death carried off, to her mother's sorrow. If she was to be unhappy, it is better that I mourn her dead, in the hope of going to meet her in heaven. But I did not love her more, nor perhaps so much, as this child, whom I held at the font, and of whom God made me a mother..."

The baroness fell silent; she had heard steps in the corridor.

Guiomar, though she had gone to dress and adorn herself, had done so with such simple means that she did not belie that morning disarray in which the reader saw her in the previous chapter. The hairstyle was a caprice of her own, expressly invented to set off at once the abundance of her hair and the womanly beauty of her brow. The embroidered points of a cambric collar folded coquettishly over the bluish tint of her glacé dress, cut and trimmed with an artistic simplicity. This, and little more, was the whole frame of the picture, one of the loveliest pictures there were in those times on the whole Praia de Botafogo.

"Long live my queen of England!" exclaimed Mrs. Oswald when she saw her appear at the door of the little sitting room.

And Guiomar smiled with so much satisfaction and enjoyment on hearing this familiar greeting that an attentive observer would have hesitated to say whether it was simple girlish vanity, or something more.

The baroness fixed her eyes on her goddaughter, loving and sad eyes, which the girl noticed, and which made her serious for a few swift seconds. But she smiled afterward; and taking her godmother's hands she gave her two kisses on the cheek, so tenderly and so sincerely that the good lady smiled with contentment.

"You need not speak," said Guiomar, "I already know you think me pretty. It is what you tell me every day, at the risk of ruining me, because if I end up vain, goodbye to my commissions, no one will be able to manage me."

Guiomar said this with so much grace and simplicity that her godmother could not help laughing, and the melancholy vanished altogether. The breakfast bell called them to other cares, and us too, friendly reader. While the three have breakfast, let us cast our eyes on the past, and see who this Guiomar was, so gentle, so sought after and so singular, as Mrs. Oswald said.
