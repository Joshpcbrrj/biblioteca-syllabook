# 5. Girlhood

Guiomar had been born humble; she was the daughter of a subordinate clerk in I know not which department of the State, an honest man, who died when she was only seven, leaving to his widow the care of educating and supporting her. The widow was an energetic and resolute woman; she dried her tears on the sleeve of her modest dress, looked the situation in the face and determined on struggle and victory.

Guiomar's godmother did not fail them in that hard trial, and looked after them as she understood to be her duty. Her solicitude, however, was not at first as constant as it came to be afterward; other family cares claimed her attention.

Even as a little girl Guiomar foretold the graces which time unfolded and perfected in her. She was a charming and delicate little creature, quite intelligent and lively, a little mischievous, certainly, but much less so than is usual in childhood. Her mother, after her husband died, had no other care on earth, nor any other ambition, than to see her accomplished and happy. She herself taught her to read badly, as she herself read, and to sew and embroider, and the little else she possessed of her woman's trade. Guiomar had no difficulty at all in retaining what her mother taught her, and applied herself to learning with such tenacity that the widow, at least in that respect, felt fortunate. "You shall be my doctor," she would say to her many a time; and this simple expression of tenderness gladdened the child and served as an incentive to her diligence.

The house they lived in was naturally modest. There her childhood ran its course, but a solitary one, which is somewhat graver. The mother, when she saw her absorbed in the games proper to her age, childishly merry, but with a merriment that hurt a mother's eyes, so deeply did that way of living pain her, the mother sometimes felt tears leap from her eyes. The daughter did not see them, because she knew how to hide them; but she guessed them through the sadness that remained on her face. She did not guess the reason, but it was enough that they were her mother's sorrows for her own gaiety to fall as well.

In time another cause of sadness loomed for the poor widow, still more painful than the first. At the age of only ten, Guiomar had certain fits of absence of spirit, certain days of concentration and muteness, a seriousness, at first intermittent and rare, then frequent and prolonged, which belied her childhood and led her mother to believe they were omens that God was calling her to himself. Today we know they were not. Might it have been the effect of that solitary and austere life, which was already shaping her soul and, as it were, refining her strength for the struggles of life?

The first time this gravity of the child became plain to her was one afternoon when she had been playing in the yard of the house. The back wall had a wide crack, through which part of the grounds belonging to a neighboring house could be seen. The crack was recent; and Guiomar had grown used to going there to rest her eyes, already serious and pensive. That afternoon, as she was looking at the mango trees, perhaps coveting the sweet yellow fruit hanging from their branches, she suddenly saw appear before her, five or six paces from where she stood, a band of girls, all pretty, who trailed their dresses among the trees and made the jewels that adorned them glitter in the last rays of the setting sun. They passed by cheerful, carefree, happy; one or another perhaps granted her some small caress; but they were gone, and with them the eyes of the interesting little girl, who remained there a long while absorbed, outside herself, still seeing in memory the picture that had passed.

Night came, the child withdrew pensive and melancholy, explaining nothing to her mother's solicitous curiosity. What could she have explained, when she could scarcely understand the impression things left upon her? But as her mother grew sad over it, Guiomar mastered her own spirit and made herself as jovial as on the best days.

This was yet another trait of the child; she had a strength of will beyond her years. With it, and the intellectual vivacity God had given her, she managed to learn everything her mother had taught her, and better than her mother knew it, once time allowed her to develop the first elements.

At thirteen she was left an orphan; this deep blow to her heart was the first she could truly feel, and the greatest that fortune dealt her. By then her godmother had already placed her in a school, where she perfected what she knew and where they taught her much besides.

The baroness's daughter was then still alive, an interesting child of thirteen, who was all her mother's soul and delight. Guiomar visited her godmother's house; the almost equal age of the two girls, the affection that bound them, Guiomar's beauty and gentleness, the graceful composure of her manners, all tightened between godmother and goddaughter the purely spiritual bonds that had united them before. Guiomar returned the feelings of that second mother; there was perhaps in her affection, though sincere, a certain heightening that might seem pretense. The affection was spontaneous; the heightening was what may have been voluntary.

The girl was sixteen when she passed to the school of Estêvão's aunt, where it seemed to the baroness she could be given a more refined education. Guiomar had then expressed the wish to be a teacher.

"There is no other resource," she told the baroness when she confided this aspiration to her.

"How so?" asked her godmother.

"There is none," Guiomar repeated. "I do not doubt, nor can I deny, the love you have for me; but each of us has an obligation, which must be fulfilled. Mine is... is to earn my bread."

These last words passed her lips as if by force. The blush rose to her cheeks; one would have said that her soul was covering her face with shame.

"Guiomar!" exclaimed the baroness.

"I ask you for something honorable for me," Guiomar answered plainly.

Her godmother smiled and approved her with a kiss, an assent of the mouth, to which the heart no longer responded, and which destiny was to change.

Shortly afterward the baroness suffered the almost mortal blow alluded to in the previous chapter. Her daughter died suddenly, and the unexpectedness of the disaster almost carried the mother to the grave.

Guiomar's affection did not belie itself in that sorrowful situation. No one showed more feeling than she at the death of Henriqueta, no one comforted so devotedly the unhappy woman who survived her. Her years were still green; yet she revealed the possession of a soul equally tender and energetic, affectionate and resolute. For some days Guiomar was the true mistress of the house; the catastrophe had cast down even Mrs. Oswald.

The poor mother's heart had been left so empty, and life seemed to her so bitter and desolate without her daughter, that she would perhaps have died of longing, had it not been for the presence of Guiomar. No other creature could fill, as this one did, the place of Henriqueta. Guiomar was already half the baroness's daughter; circumstances, no less than the heart, had destined them for each other.

One day when the goddaughter had gone to visit her godmother, the latter told her that she would soon come to fetch her to her house.

"You will be the daughter I lost; she did not love me more, nor would I now have any other consolation."

"Oh! godmother!" exclaimed Guiomar, kissing her hands.

The baroness was seated; Guiomar knelt at her feet and laid her head in her lap. The good mother bent down and kissed it tenderly, with her eyes on that daughter whom events had given her, and her thoughts in heaven, where the other must be, whom God had given her and taken to himself.

Shortly afterward Guiomar settled definitively in her godmother's house, where gladness gradually revived, thanks to the new resident, in whom there was a rare tact and sagacity. Having witnessed for some time, and not a brief one, the way of life between her godmother and Henriqueta, Guiomar put all her effort into reproducing in the same manner the habits of the former time, so that the baroness could scarcely feel the absence of her daughter. She forgot none of the other's attentions, and if she altered them at some point it was to add new ones. This intention did not escape the baroness's mind, and it is superfluous to say that in this way the bonds of affection drew tighter between them.

While she was proving the feelings of her heart, the girl revealed no less the full harmony of her instincts with the society she had entered. The education she had received in recent times did much, but not everything. Nature had undertaken to complete the work, or rather, to begin it. No one would have guessed, from the finely elegant manners of that girl, the modest origin she had had; the butterfly made one forget the chrysalis.
