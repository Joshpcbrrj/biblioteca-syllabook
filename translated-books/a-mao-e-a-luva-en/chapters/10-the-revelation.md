# 10. The Revelation

Half an hour later, as she was about to open the book to continue reading, Guiomar saw Jorge's little note. It had no envelope; it was a mere folded slip of paper, redolent of love. Guiomar's mind was so far from any such thing that she suspected nothing and absently opened it. The first word written was her name; the last was Jorge's.

Guiomar's first gesture was one of anger. Had he been able to spy on her through the keyhole and see the expression of her face, it is very likely that all the love he had nourished until now would have turned into vexation. But he was not there, and the girl could faithfully translate onto her face the movements of her heart.

"One more," she thought; "this one, however..."

And this time the gesture was not one of anger, but of something more, half weariness, half pity, a difficult and rare mixture.

The girl stayed quiet for some time, looking at the paper without wanting to read it, as if hesitating between burning it and returning it untouched to its author. But curiosity won in the end; Guiomar unfolded the paper and read these lines:

> "Guiomar! Forgive me if I call you so; social conventions condemn me, certainly, but my heart approves, what do I say? it itself writes these letters. It is not my pen, it is not my lips that speak to you in this way, it is all the living forces of my existence, which proclaim aloud the immense and profound love I have for you.
>
> Before you read it on this paper, you must already have seen it, or at least guessed it in my eyes, in the sweet intoxication that the presence of yours produces in me. I am persuaded that all my effort to suppress this affection is vain; however sincerely I may wish to forget you, I shall never succeed; I shall achieve only a new affliction. The remorse of trying will come to crown my other misfortunes.
>
> Why do I break today the silence I have kept, a timid and respectful silence which, if it does not open to me the road of glory, at least preserves for me the palm of hope? I could not myself answer you; I speak because an inner force commands me to speak, as the river overflows, as light spreads; I speak because I should perhaps die if I kept silent, just as I shall die of despair if, besides the pardon I ask of you, you do not give me a hope more certain than this one, which makes me live and consume myself.
>
> Jorge."

Guiomar read this letter twice, one reading of curiosity, the other of analysis and reflection, and at the end of the second she found herself as cold as before the first. She looked for some time at the paper, and in thought at the man who had written it; at last she set the letter aside, opened the book, and went on with the novel.

But her mind, which had not remained as indifferent as her heart, began to flee from the novel toward life, with such tenacity that there was no help for it but that her eyes should follow, and the girl plunged again into the reflections suggested to her by the case of Jorge's passion.

It was not a passion, or would not be at least in the full sense of the word; but something less, or like it, and even so real, and Guiomar saw clearly that it could become one. But how far would her adorer go if she slighted him at once; and, given the baroness's love for her nephew, how far would the refusal wound her? Guiomar swept from her mind the fears born of such questions; but she felt them first, she weighed them before putting them away, which will reveal to the reader in what proportion feeling and reason, the tendencies of the soul and the calculations of life, were combined in her.

The fear excluded, laughter came back to her, that inward laughter, which is the most involuntary and cruel, and also the least risky that one can bestow on human fatuities. The love of a man whose absurdity was compensated by some good qualities, and who was, after all, also distinguished, though his distinction ran rather to a lacy and complicated style, which is not the best, could not be so contemptible as all that. Guiomar saw all this, and on the other hand she could not prevent his loving her; yet she found that confession no less rash.

The girl reflected too on the special position the baroness's nephew had in that house; she saw herself obliged to his presence, and perhaps to a struggle, because the suitor would not retreat at the first blow. There were no such fears on Estêvão's side; she acknowledged that his passion was ardent and profound, and therefore more capable of follies; but she compared the natures of the two men, and if both seemed to her of feeble moral constitution, she nonetheless recognized that the bachelor lacked a certain presumption that distinguished the other, and with which she might have to contend.

When she made this comparison between the two men, her eyes grew a little softer and more languid, the work of three minutes only, but three minutes which, had Estêvão known of them, he would have traded the rest of his whole life for. And yet it was not love or longing; some sympathy, yes, though slight and of no consequence; but above all it was regret at being unable to love him, or better still, it was pity that such a heart had not been wedded to another mind.

Guiomar reflected still much and long, and she did not only reflect, she also daydreamed, letting out all the sail of that swift schooner of the imagination on which we all voyage at some time in life, when firm and hard land tires us, and the vast sea without shores calls to us. Her imagination, however, was neither morbid nor romantic nor mawkish, nor did it take her to gather flowers in wild regions or fall asleep beside blue lakes. She was nothing of the sort and did nothing of the sort; and however far she sailed, she would carry deep in her soul the memories of the land.

She turned back at last, and her eyes fell upon the letter. Present reality could not have shown itself to her in a worse light. Guiomar rose irritated, seized the paper and crumpled it feverishly; perhaps she was about to tear it up, when she heard a soft knock at the door.

"Who is it?" she asked.

"It is I," answered Mrs. Oswald's voice.

The girl went to open the door; the Englishwoman entered dressed for bed, with a lively astonishment in her eyes that seemed to take her voice away for some seconds. Guiomar, alarmed, asked:

"What is it? Has something happened to my godmother?"

"Heaven forbid!" exclaimed the Englishwoman. "Nothing has happened to her; the baroness, naturally, sleeps soundly. I come because from my room I thought I heard the sound of steps here, and then I saw a light. I thought you might be unwell. But, from what I see," the Englishwoman went on, casting her eyes on the little table where the open book lay, "from what I see, you have not yet finished reading your novel..."

"I have not read a line since I retired," answered Guiomar, fixing her eyes on the Englishwoman's face, as if seized by a sudden thought.

"Truly!"

"I read something else," the girl went on; "I read this paper."

Mrs. Oswald bent forward to read the paper too, having guessed what it was; Guiomar flung it onto the table.

"There is no need," she said; "it is a declaration of love."

"From whom?" asked the Englishwoman, opening astonished and obedient eyes.

"Read the name."

Mrs. Oswald read the signature of the letter, which the girl presented to her again.

"Naturally," Guiomar continued, "there is some work of yours in this..."

"Mine!" interrupted the other, a little more sharply than she was used to speak.

Guiomar had gone to sit down; her little foot beat impatiently on the carpet with a quick and regular movement, her arms were folded across her chest, and she fixed the Englishwoman with eyes in which one could read the keen exacerbation of her spirit. A brief silence followed; Mrs. Oswald drew up another chair and sat down near the girl.

"Why must you be unjust to me?" she said, giving her voice a honeyed and pleading tone; "why will you not see things as they naturally are? What there is in this is a curious coincidence, but nothing more. If I spoke to you of such a matter a few times, it was because I myself perceived Mr. Jorge's love for you; it is something everyone sees. I imagined that marriage, in this case, would be agreeable to the baroness, to whom I am grateful. I may have done wrong..."

"Very wrong," interrupted Guiomar; "these are family matters in which you have no business."

Guiomar rose again, took a few steps, and sat down again. With the movement her hair came loose and fell over her shoulders. Mrs. Oswald approached to gather and tie it, but the girl curtly repelled her:

"Leave it, leave it..."

And she herself rearranged it with her slender little hands, and then sat looking at the floor, biting her lip, breathing hard, as if holding back a word that strove to burst out, impetuous and angry. Mrs. Oswald said nothing for some minutes; she waited for the acute period of the irritation to pass. When it seemed to her that Guiomar had relaxed, she at last broke the silence.

"I did wrong, I did, there is no doubt, but the intention could not have been better. Perhaps you do not believe me; patience! What I ask of you, I do not even ask it, what I firmly believe is that you will not attribute to me some interest of a kind..."

Mrs. Oswald paused to give Guiomar room to protest, but Guiomar did not protest, I mean she did not protest aloud; she made only a gesture of denial, enough to satisfy the Englishwoman's sensibilities. The girl was sincere; she really did not attribute to any base, any pecuniary interest, Mrs. Oswald's action. Yet she did not absolve her for that, not only because she might contribute to a painful crisis, but also, and it is well to note it again, because the Englishwoman's position in that house was relatively inferior.

The Englishwoman went on speaking in her own defense, justifying minutely the good feelings of her heart, and promising to drop the whole affair, which in her judgment was the best thing the girl could do.

"Experience of life," she concluded, "ought to have convinced me that the best of all feelings is a quiet and silent selfishness."

While she spoke thus, Guiomar seemed to return to her habitual tranquillity. The change was, not sudden, but somewhat quicker than it should have been, in a mind such as hers, in which impressions were neither superficial nor momentary. There were even touches of affability in her face and voice when she began to speak, which perhaps revealed that change to be very voluntary and considered.

"Very well, Mrs. Oswald, what is past is past. I am sorry that things came to this point, and that he thought of writing such a letter, confessing a passion that I believe sincere, but to which my heart cannot respond. Loves are not ordered like dresses; above all they are not feigned, or ought never to be feigned."

"Oh! certainly!"

"I am fond of him, as a relative of my godmother's, and also because she has a mother's affection for him, as for me; we are a sort of brother and sister, nothing more."

"You are quite right," assented Mrs. Oswald. "You think and speak like a doctor. What is there to be done? Whoever does not love does not love. It is he I pity!"

"He is very fond of me, is he not?" asked Guiomar, fixing her eyes on the Englishwoman.

"Oh! it seems so! You must know it as well as I; I know what I have seen, and I believe it is a great deal."

"I have never seen anything," answered Guiomar dryly.

Mrs. Oswald's answer was a smile of incredulity, which the other did not see or did not wish to see. There was a pause; Guiomar went on in these terms:

"But however that may be, my answer is no. I trust he will not do me the injury of wanting to marry me without my loving him..."

Guiomar stopped, as if waiting for the other to say something. This time it fell to Mrs. Oswald to answer nothing, neither with voice nor with gesture. The girl leaned forward, rested her arms on her knees, fingers interlaced, and, between an amiable smile and an affectionate look, went on:

"You could, if he has ever spoken to you of this or comes to speak to you, you could dissuade him from such ideas, simply telling him the truth and giving him advice, the advice that you will know how to give, and that he will surely accept, because he has a good heart, an estimable character..."

"Oh! excellent! an excellent young man!"

And the two sat looking at each other. Guiomar smiling, but with a smile that was a voluntary contraction of the muscles, and the Englishwoman wearing a face of pity, and adoration, and sorrow, and many things together, which the girl began to understand only when the other broke the silence in this way:

"I am wondering whether I ought to tell you the rest."

"The rest?" asked Guiomar, surprised. "Why, is there more?"

The Englishwoman drew her chair closer. Guiomar straightened her bust and waited anxiously for the revelation, if revelation it was, that Mrs. Oswald was going to make. She did not speak at once; it was reasonable to hesitate a little, to struggle with herself, before saying something. At last, with the movement of one who gathers all her strength and applies it to something beyond her usual courage:

"Dona Guiomar," she said, taking her hands, "no one can demand that you marry without loving the bridegroom; it would truly be an affront. But what I tell you is that the love that does not exist for the present may come later, and if it came, it would be a great fortune..."

"But finish, finish," interrupted the girl impatiently.

"It would be a great fortune for you, for him, I dare say for me, who esteem and adore you both, but above all for the baroness."

"How so?" said Guiomar.

"Oh! for her it would be the greatest fortune of her life, for it is today her most deeply rooted and lively wish, her wish truly of the soul. You..."

"Are you certain of that?"

"Most certain."

"I do not believe it, I see nothing that..."

"Believe it, you must believe it. If you promise to say nothing of this conversation of ours, nor to let her suspect in any way what I am telling you..."

"Speak."

"Well then," Mrs. Oswald went on, lowering her voice, as if someone could hear her in the solitude of that bedchamber and the deep silence of that house, which was all asleep, "well then, I will tell you that I learned of this wish of hers from her own lips. When I perceived Mr. Jorge's passion, I spoke of it to your godmother, jesting, in the intimacy she allows me, and the baroness, instead of smiling, as I expected her to do, remained for some time pensive and serious, until she broke out in these words: 'Oh! if Guiomar were fond of him and they came to marry, I should be completely happy. I have no other ambition on earth now. It shall be my campaign.'"

"My godmother said that?" asked Guiomar.

"Just so. The answer I gave her was that the marriage was not impossible, and that nothing was more natural than for two people at first indifferent to come to love each other. Love is born many times from habit."

Guiomar scarcely heard what the Englishwoman was saying to her; if she still looked at her, it was with undecided and clouded eyes, like one wholly absorbed in inner thoughts.

"It was from that day," Mrs. Oswald continued, "that it seemed to me proper to speak to you of it a few times, to sound your heart, to see whether it favored your godmother's dream, making all this house happy... I did wrong, I agree; but the intention was the most respectable and holy in this world."

"Certainly," murmured Guiomar.

Mrs. Oswald took one of her hands and kissed it affectionately. Guiomar did not repel her nor even seem to heed the Englishwoman's tenderness. The two looked at each other for a few brief minutes, saying nothing, as if reading in each other's souls.

Guiomar had neither the experience nor the age of the Englishwoman, who might have been her mother; but experience and age were replaced, as the reader knows, by great natural tact and sagacity. There are creatures who reach fifty without ever passing fifteen, so simple, so blind, so green has nature made them; for these the twilight is a prolongation of the dawn. Others not; they ripen in due season like flowers; they come into the world with the wrinkle of reflection in the mind, though without prejudice to feeling, which lives and works in them but does not rule. In these the heart is born bridled; it trots wide, goes at a walk, or gallops, as a heart does, but never bolts, never loses itself or loses its rider.

What the baroness's goddaughter sought to read in Mrs. Oswald's face was whether her godmother did in fact nourish that wish, or whether such a revelation was no more than a trick. The reader knows it was true; but he will admit, no doubt, that the girl gave it credence only after much questioning and examining. She believed at last; she believed because it was plausible, she believed because the Englishwoman would not risk any indiscretion on her part, which would unmask her completely.

"It seems to me," said Mrs. Oswald, "that I did not do wrong in telling you all I knew. Advice I give you none; the best of it is not worth the voice of your own heart. Yours is pure and upright; consult it willingly, and you will see whether there is indifference in it, or whether some spark..."

"I know!" interrupted Guiomar. "It never occurred to me to consult it."

"You do wrong; it is the clock of life. Whoever does not consult it naturally goes out of time. But what do I see!" continued Mrs. Oswald, casting her eyes on Guiomar's little clock. "On that other clock it is ten minutes to one! One o'clock! What would the baroness say if she knew we were still here talking! I withdraw; may God give you a quiet sleep, and above all make you happy, as you deserve. I do not recommend prudence to you, for you have more than enough. Good night, until tomorrow."

And Mrs. Oswald went out on tiptoe toward her room.

Guiomar remained alone, seated there at the foot of the bed, listening to the muffled and cautious step of the Englishwoman. When the sound died away entirely, and the silence of the night returned to what it was, deep and sepulchral, the girl let her arms fall upon the bed and her head into her hands, and a sigh was torn from her breast, long, loud, wounded, the first the reader hears from her since he has known her, and at last these words wrung from her soul, so pained, I was about to say so tearful, came forth:

"Oh, my dreams! my dreams!"

She did not weep; hers was one of those souls that have no tears while strength remains to them. Her eyes were dry and steady when she raised them from her hands; her face bore traces of the shock, but there was no dejection in it, still less despair.
