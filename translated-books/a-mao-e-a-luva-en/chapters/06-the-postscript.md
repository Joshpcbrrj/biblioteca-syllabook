# 6. The Postscript

That counsel of Luís Alves, on the fatal night two years before, was beyond doubt a judicious one, and ought to have stayed in Estêvão's mind. It was not advisable to reread the letter, on pain of finding a postscript in it. But Estêvão was curious about epistles; he could not keep himself from opening that one. The postscript was there, at the end.

To put it in plain language, Estêvão left Luís Alves's garden with his heart half inclined to love again the woman who had once made him suffer so much. Someone will conclude from this that he had never truly stopped loving her. It may be so; perhaps there was beneath the ashes a spark, a single one, and that was enough to kindle the fire again. But however it may have been, it is certain that Estêvão left that place with the beginning of love in his heart.

All that day was one of agitation and tumult for him, and he did not resign himself at once, but rather sought to resist the entry of the new passion. The attempt was sincere; it was his strength that was scant. He turned the girl's image away from him; but she pursued him, tenacious as a remorse, fatal as the voice of his destiny.

Estêvão said nothing to Luís Alves of the meeting and of the conversation he had had with the girl in the garden; and he did not hide it from distrust, but from shame. But what could he have told him that Luís Alves had not already seen and understood? From the window of his room, which looked out on the garden, peering through the chink in the curtains, he had been able to watch them during those three quarters of an hour of innocent chat. The spectacle did not much amuse him; Luís Alves thought the choice of place a little bold.

The sight of them together drew his attention to the coincidence of the neighbor's name with that of his colleague's old sweetheart; it was naturally the same person.

"He is going to tell me everything," thought Luís Alves, when he saw his colleague move away from the fence and turn his steps toward the house.

Estêvão, as I said, was discreet. He came in preoccupied, very different from the man who had gone out the evening before, with something more serious to be read in his face than he himself was wont to be.

Estêvão had the past and the future against him. The present, to be sure, defended him; he felt that something kept him at a distance from Guiomar. But the past spoke to him of all sweet remembrances, the least bitter ones, and memory scarcely knows of others when it recalls what was. The future beckoned to him with all its hopes, and it is enough to say that they were infinite. Besides, the Guiomar he now saw rose before him in the midst of another atmosphere, the very one his spirit longed to breathe; and she appeared to him only to flee at once. Above all this stood the obstacle, that closed door, which might well be that of the città dolente, but which in any case he would have liked to see thrown open to his ambitions.

The days ran by, alternating between confidence and dejection, woven of gold and black thread, a struggle of every hour, which ended as it was to be foreseen and as it was bound to end. His heart carried Estêvão off after it.

He neglected no means within reach to see Guiomar. The windows of the house were almost always deserted. Two or three times he happened to see her from afar; as he drew near, the figure vanished into the shadow of the drawing room. He missed no theater; but only twice did he have the pleasure of seeing her: once at the Lírico, where the Sonnambula was being sung, once at the Ginásio, where the Parisians were being played, without his hearing a note of the opera or a word of the comedy. All of him, eyes and thought, was in Guiomar's box. At the Lírico that contemplation was in vain; the girl did not notice him. At the Ginásio she did; the theater was small; yet he might as well have gone unseen, so stubbornly did she turn her eyes away from the place where he sat.

Yet Estêvão did not fail to go and wait for her at the exit, to place himself frankly in her path, to solicit boldly her eyes and attention. The family came down from the second tier by the stairs on the São Francisco side; the narrowness of the place was excellent. On the baroness's arm was a young man of twenty-five, an elegant figure, though somewhat affected. All three came down and waited a few minutes for the carriage. In the half-shadow that was there, Guiomar's marble face and the grace of her figure stood out. Her great eyes wandered over the crowd, but rested on no one. She possessed, as no other did, the art of enjoying, without seeming to see them, the homages of public admiration.

Irritated by the girl's indifference, Estêvão wandered all that night, alone with his spite and his love, weaving and unweaving a thousand plans, each more absurd than the last. The cup had filled to the brim; it had to be poured out upon the breast of a friend, of a friend who held in his hands the one remedy he asked for at that moment: the key to that door.

Luís Alves was that man.

"Fallen again!" he exclaimed, laughing, when Estêvão told him everything. "I had already noticed it. Women, you know... So you want me to take you there?"

"I do."

Luís Alves reflected for a few moments.

"And a journey: would it not do you good to take a journey? I know what you are going to say to me; but I am not proposing a pleasure trip to Europe either. Look, I will arrange for you, if you like, a post as municipal judge..."

The proposal was sincere; Estêvão thought he saw in it a hint of mockery and shrugged his shoulders in annoyance. The proposal, however, deserved to be examined; it was a career, and it came from a man who was entering political life, who expected in a few weeks the result of an election, with the certainty, or near enough, of having triumphed. It was an influence being born, and it would grow in strength. But for Estêvão, at that moment, every public career, influence, future, laws, everything lay in Guiomar's brown eyes.

"I love her," he said at last, "that is everything to me. It may well be that you are right; perhaps some great sorrow awaits me; but those are reflections, and I do not reflect now, I feel..."

"In any case," Luís Alves broke in, "I am performing my duty as a friend; I tell you that the two of you were not born for each other; that if she did not love you in those days, she will love you much less today, and that, in short..."

Luís Alves stopped short.

"In short?" asked Estêvão.

"In short, you are asking me for a sacrifice," concluded the lawyer, laughing, "because I too once courted her a little... There is no need to knit your brow; it was a neighbor's courtship, an attempt that lasted little more than twenty-four hours. I say it with shame: she did not pay me a crumb of attention, and I went back to my case files."

"Then... you are fond of her?" asked Estêvão.

"I find her pretty, and nothing more. It was throwing mud at a wall; had she accepted, I would have married; she did not accept..."

"You see that we are different."

"So you want it?..."

"A friend's service."

"Well," said Luís Alves at last, "let your will be done. The baroness is about to take care of a lawsuit and has sent for me to speak. I will pass the prebend on to you; you will go in there as a lawyer, which in some measure takes a weight off my conscience."

Estêvão, who asked only for a pretext, accepted the offer with both hands, and thanked him with such effusive tenderness that it made the other smile.

The promise was kept punctually. Luís Alves introduced Estêvão to the baroness the following night, as his companion and friend, as a lawyer capable of watching over the interests of the illustrious client. The reception was generally good, except on Guiomar's part, who seemed annoyed to see him in that house. When Estêvão greeted her, as one who had known her for a long time, she could scarcely return his greeting; for the whole rest of the evening she did not say a word to him. On that side the welcome could not have been worse; but Estêvão felt happy, so long as he could see her and breathe the same air, asking nothing more for the present, and leaving the rest to fortune.

Of all the people in the baroness's house, the first to notice the indifference with which Guiomar treated Estêvão was Mrs. Oswald. The shrewd Englishwoman buckled on the most impassive mask she had brought from the British Isles and did not lose sight of them. Neither the first time nor the second did she see anything but his eyes, which solicited hers, and hers, which seemed deaf. There was certainly a passion, solitary and unheeded.

"Do you know that I have discovered an admirer of yours?" she asked Guiomar some days later.

Guiomar made a gesture of surprise.

"Let us understand each other," observed the Englishwoman; "I do not say that you court him in return; I say that it is he who is in love. Can you not guess?"

"Perhaps."

"Dr. Estêvão."

Guiomar made a gesture of disdain.

"I see that you had guessed," said Mrs. Oswald; "it was not difficult either. Anyone with some practice in these things smells a passion a hundred leagues away, however much it seeks to hide from strange eyes. Lovers generally suppose that no one sees them; it is a pity. Look, of you I can swear that you are in love with no one."

"What do you know of that?" asked Guiomar, casting her eyes on the mirror of her wardrobe. "Why, I am, but with myself."

Mrs. Oswald burst out laughing, a grave and measured laugh. She knew that the girl was proud of her graces; it was a good way to flatter her feeling. She said many pretty things to her, which do not come in here, and concluded by placing her hands on her shoulders, looking her straight in the eye, and finally breaking out in these words, half sighed:

"You are the flower of this land of yours. Who will pluck you? I know someone who deserves you..."

Guiomar grew serious, and gently pushed the Englishwoman's hands away, murmuring:

"Mrs. Oswald, let us speak of something else."
