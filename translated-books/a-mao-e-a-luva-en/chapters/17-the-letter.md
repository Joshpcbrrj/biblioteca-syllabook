# 17. The Letter

There was no need to reread the paper to understand it; but loving eyes delight in enamored letters. The paper held a single word: "Ask me", written in the center of the sheet, in a fine, elegant, feminine hand. Luís Alves looked at the note for some time, first as a lover, then as a mere observer. The handwriting was not trembling, but it seemed to have been flung upon the paper in an hour of emotion.

From this observation Luís Alves passed to a very natural reflection. That note, hardly proper in any other circumstances, was justified by the declaration he himself had made to the girl some days before, when he asked her to get to know him first, and said that on the day she judged him worthy to take him for her husband, he would listen to her and follow. But if this was so with regard to the note, it was not so with regard to the hour. What motive could oblige the girl to throw him that decisive paper from the window, at midnight, eloquent in the very sobriety with which she had written it?

Luís Alves concluded that there was some urgent reason, and therefore that he must meet the situation with the means the situation called for. As to the reason itself, he could not discover it. The fact, plain enough, of the court the baroness's nephew was paying to Guiomar occurred to him, but he was ignorant of the circumstances concerning it, and could go no further.

I shall not say that Luís Alves spent the night digging deep in the ground of vague conjectures. He was not a man to waste time on useless things; and nothing was more useless on that occasion than trying to explain what for him could have no explanation. What he resolved was to obey the girl's message: to ask for her without hesitation or preamble. But if the case did not cause him insomnia, it did extend his vigil beyond the usual hour, as was fitting on so solemn an occasion, above all in the matter of a creature who in those days was the envy and the covetousness of many eyes. Luís Alves was not like Estêvão, an adorable dreamer; he did not feed on imaginings and reveries, a food that nourishes little or nothing; but he mused for some time, steeped himself for an hour in the ideal contemplation of the woman he had known how to choose. Sleep came, and the reverie mingled with the dream.

Did Guiomar sleep as restfully as he? She slept; but her night had been far more agitated and bitter, as was natural after Jorge's declaration and her godmother's insinuations.

The girl had retired to her room right after the declaration. The people of the house could read nothing in her face, save a sudden pallor and the blush that followed it; but as soon as she found herself alone, she gave full vent to the feelings she had till then been able to contain.

The first of them was resentment; Guiomar felt humiliated by that declaration, made thus, by ambush and surprise, to wrest from her a consent which her heart and her nature rejected. No consultation, no prior authorization; it seemed to her that they treated her as an absolutely passive being, without will or choice of her own, destined to satisfy the caprices of others. Her godmother's words belied this supposition; but the knowledge she had of the baroness's resolution in this affair greatly diminished the worth of such words. If it was a campaign, as Mrs. Oswald had said, they wanted to constrain her with appearances of moderation, and the time they left her to reflect was really time to consider, alone with herself, the necessity of repaying the benefits she had received.

Let no one accuse her of having made these reflections as soon as she entered the room, with sparkling eyes and lips cold with anger. They were natural: first because she supposed that her marriage to Jorge was decided and would take place whatever the circumstances; then because her soul was touchy; she did not forget the benefits received, but she would have wished not to be reminded of them by means of a violence: to do so was the same as to throw them in her face.

"No!" the girl murmured at last. "To force me, to reduce me to the condition of a mere servant, never."

But this anger subsided, and heart prevailed over heart. Guiomar recalled the baroness's constant tenderness toward her, the solicitude with which she satisfied her smallest wishes, which were orders there, and she could not reconcile so great a love with the supposed violence that was to be done her. She was not slow to repent of the incoherent words that had escaped her, and of the evil feelings she had attributed to the baroness's heart. She crossed her hands on her breast and raised her thought to heaven, as if to ask its pardon. Guiomar, amid the seductions of life, which were so many for her and wholly drew her eyes, had not lost the religious sentiment, nor forgotten what the naïve and pure faith of her mother had taught her.

The anger was over, but then came the struggle between gratitude and love, between the suitor her godmother's affection proposed and the one her own heart had chosen. She dared neither take away the baroness's hopes nor sacrifice her own, and one of the two things she had to do on that solemn occasion. What she felt and thought was long and cruel; but if such a duel could be waged in her soul, the outcome was not doubtful. The outcome had to be one. Will and ambition, when they truly dominate, may struggle with other feelings, but they must always win, because they are the weapons of the strong, and victory belongs to the strong. Guiomar had to decide between the two men her destiny proposed to her; she chose the one who spoke to her heart.

The answer, however, the girl could neither delay nor evade; it was perhaps not advisable to prolong the struggle and the doubt. When she thought this, a decisive idea came to her mind: to confess everything to her godmother. She hesitated, however, between doing so herself or through Luís Alves, whose words, noted above, she carried written in her memory. She preferred this means; but preferring it was not enough, it had to be carried out, and for that she had only two ways, to write to him or to speak to him.

The second might not be so prompt, and perhaps no suitable occasion would come; she adopted the first, and at once drew back. The letter would be sent by a servant, but Guiomar's spirit was so much her own that she rejected any such intervention. The window was open; from it she saw a light in Luís Alves's room and the young man's shadow, as he walked from one side to the other. The idea then occurred to her that she put into practice, as was told in the previous chapter.

Such is the story of that word written hastily on a sheet of paper. In spite of Luís Alves's declaration and the circumstances in which the girl found herself, the reader will easily understand that she did not write it without battling with herself, without wavering greatly between repugnance and necessity. In the end the scruples were overcome, which is so often their fate, and it must be said that they are never overcome for nothing, because they speak, reason, obstruct as much as they can, but it is common for them to be walked over. The girl, however, had no sooner thrown the letter than she repented; her dignity had remorse; her conscience almost accused her of a base action.

It was late; the letter had reached its destination.

The next morning the baroness woke merrier than usual. She had thought she saw in Guiomar, the night before, something that seemed to her only the natural embarrassment of the situation. Guiomar rose late; the morning was rainy and her godmother did not take her walk. The girl went to kiss her hand and her cheek, as she was accustomed, and to receive from her the maternal kiss. Her face seemed tired, but a veil of affected gaiety disguised its natural expression, like the paints of a dressing table, so that the baroness, a poor reader of faces, did not discern in this one the truth from the imposture. Imposture, I say, though it must be understood to be honest and upright, because the girl's intention was no more than not to embitter her godmother and to remove from her any cause for premature affliction.

"Did my queen of England sleep well?" asked Mrs. Oswald, putting her hands familiarly on her shoulders.

"Your queen of England has no crown," answered Guiomar with a forced smile.

Around noon the baroness received a letter from Luís Alves. She opened it and read it. The lawyer was asking her for Guiomar's hand. A few lines, courteous, simple, natural, written by one who seemed master of the situation.

"Mrs. Oswald," said the baroness to her companion, who was in the same room, "read this."

The Englishwoman obeyed.

"This means nothing," she observed after a few moments. "It is one suitor more; we must believe, however, that they are many, and that if the others do not write her letters like this, it is because they are less bold. Does the baroness think her goddaughter's eyes are innocent?" the Englishwoman went on, smiling. "I fancy they must be laden with crimes, and that there are dead men..."

"But do you not see, Mrs. Oswald," the baroness interrupted, "that this man seems to be authorized?"

Mrs. Oswald fell silent like one reflecting. Soon after she set forth a series of arguments and considerations, if not grave in substance, at least in the clothes she dressed them in, seriously British clothes, such as the best scissors of the House of Commons could not have cut better. All of her had the air of a living argument without reply. There was in her hair, between blond and white, all the rigidity of a syllogism; each nostril seemed the point of a dilemma. The conclusion of it all was that nothing was lost, and that Jorge's happiness was not only possible but even probable, provided the baroness showed (this was the essential thing) a certain resolution of mind, very useful and even indispensable on that occasion. Mrs. Oswald offered to go and call the girl at once.

"Then go, go," said the baroness.

The Englishwoman left and went to find Guiomar. When she saw her from afar she composed a smile, and Guiomar, seeing her smile, felt as it were an inward movement of repulsion.

"I come to fetch you," said Mrs. Oswald, "for something you are far from imagining."

Guiomar questioned her with her eyes.

"To be married!"

"Married!" exclaimed Guiomar, not understanding the messenger's intention.

"Nothing less," answered the other. "You are surprised, are you not? So am I; and your godmother likewise. But there is someone with the bad taste to fall in love with your beautiful eyes, and the affront of coming to ask for you, as if one were to ask for the stars of heaven..."

Guiomar understood what it was about. She looked disdainfully at the Englishwoman and said in a dry, brief tone:

"But finish, Mrs. Oswald."

"The baroness sends for you."

Guiomar made ready to go to her godmother; Mrs. Oswald made her stop for a moment, and with the most honeyed voice she had on the scale of her throat, said:

"All the happiness of this house is in your hands."
