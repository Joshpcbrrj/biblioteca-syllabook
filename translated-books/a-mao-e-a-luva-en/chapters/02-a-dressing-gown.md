# 2. A Dressing Gown

A month after Estêvão arrived in São Paulo, his passion was definitively dead and buried, he himself singing a responsory over it, in alternating voices, with two or three young ladies of the city, all of them as a pastime. It goes without saying that two years later, when he took his bachelor's degree, no thought of the courtship on Rua dos Inválidos remained to him. Moreover, the fair Guiomar had long since left the school and gone to live with her godmother. He had not seen her the first time he came back to the Court. Now he returned a graduate in juridical and social sciences, as has been said, more eager to explore the future than to reread the past.

The Court amused itself, as it has always amused itself, more or less, and for those who have crossed the line of fifty it amused itself more than it does today, the eternal remark of those who no longer give life all the bloom of their early years. To mature men, youth never makes merry as it did in their time, which is natural to say, because each man sees things with the eyes of his age. The recreations of youth are certainly not equally noble, nor equally frivolous, in every age; but the fault or the merit is not youth's, poor youth, but rather that of the time which falls to its lot.

The Court amused itself, despite the recent ravages of the cholera; there were balls, there was singing, there were strolls, there was the theater. The Casino opened its salons, as did the Club, as did the Congress, all three Fluminense in name and in soul. These were the Homeric times of the lyric theater, the memorable season of those struggles and rivalries renewed every half year, perhaps from an excess of ardor and enthusiasm, which time has diminished, or transferred (God forgive it) to matters of lesser moment. Who does not remember, or who has not heard tell of, the battles fought in that classic pit of the theater on the Campo da Aclamação, between the Casaloni legion and the Charton phalanx, but above all between the latter and the Lagrua regiment? They were pitched battles, with fresh troops, and mature ones too, equipped with flowers, verses, wreaths, and even firecrackers. One night the action was joined between the Lagrua camp and the Charton camp with such violence that it seemed a page of the Iliad. This time the Venus of the situation came out of the combat wounded; a firecracker had burst in Charton's face. The fury, the delirium, the confusion were indescribable; applause and catcalls joined hands, and feet. The fight passed into the newspapers. "Eternal shame (said one) upon the gentlemen who spat in the face of a lady!" "If need be (replied another) we will give the names of the critics who in the lobby of the theater swore to insult Mlle. Lagrua." "Unbridled rabble!" "Bloated gentility!"

Those who survived those wars of rosemary and marjoram must feel today, eighteen years later, that they spent excessive enthusiasm on things that called for repose of spirit and a lesson in taste.

Estêvão is one of the relics of that Troy, and he was one of the most fervent Lagruists, before and after his degree. The principal cause of his preferences was certainly the singer's talent; but the one he was accustomed to give, in his hours of good humor, which were all twenty-four of the day, except those of sleep, the cause that bound him above all to the "camps of good taste," he said, was (imagine!) the down on Mlle. Lagrua's upper lip. Perhaps he was not the only amateur of that down; but a more fervent one, I doubt there was in this good city. A Machiavellian Chartonist, an elegant writer besides, raised that down to the rank of a moustache, shrewdly understanding that if down was a grace, a moustache was an excrescence; and he would not forgive Lagrua's lip even that.

"Oh! that down!" Estêvão would exclaim in the intervals of an opera. "That delicious down will be the ruin of decent people! Would that I could go curling up there, until I was nearer heaven, I mean her eyes, and be seen by her, who does not pick me out in the innumerable throng of her adorers! Do you want to know something? There is where she must keep her soul, and I should like to amuse myself with her soul, and tell it the many little things I have in here waiting for a lip of down that will hear them."

Estêvão was more or less the same man as two years before. He still smelled of the swaddling clothes of the Academy, half student and half doctor, combining in himself, as at an age of transition, the thoughtlessness of the one with the dignity of the other. He had the same chimeras, and the same simplicity of heart; only he had not shown them in the verses he printed in the academic newspapers, which were all steeped in the purest Byronism, a fashion much of the time. In them the young man confessed to the city and the world the profound disbelief of his spirit and his purely literary weariness. The conferring of the degree interrupted, or perhaps ended, that poetic vocation; the last sigh of that kind to leave his breast was a set of sextains to his lost youth.

Happily, he had lost it only in verse; in prose and in reality he was a youth like few others.

Although he had cut a good figure at the Academy, he esteemed the science of Law more than he loved it. His intellectual preferences were divided, or rather embraced Politics and Literature, and even so, Politics beckoned to him only with whatever literary element there might be in it. He had read in both, but read swiftly and on the surface of the pages. Estêvão would never have understood this axiom of Lord Macaulay, that it profits more to digest a page than to devour a volume. He digested nothing; and hence came his utter lack of attachment to the sciences he had studied. He overcame his repugnance out of self-esteem; but once he had rounded the Cape of Disciplinary Storms, he left to others the care of steering for India.

His political aspirations were bound to die in the germ, not only because he lacked the support needed to raise them up and make them bear fruit, but also because he had not in himself the strength indispensable to every man who sets his sights above the station in which he was born. They were vague, intermittent, vaporous aspirations, certain legislative and ministerial visions, which as soon courted his imagination as vanished at the first pretty eyes that glided by, for those he did love in earnest. Opinions he had none; a few writings he had published during his academic period were a medley of doctrines of every kind, which floated in his mind without ever settling, coming and going, rising or sinking, according to his latest reading or his present mood.

For now he served in the ranks of Lagruism, with the ardor, devotion and fidelity of a good apostle. He was not well-off enough to pay for the luxury of a lyrical opinion; he had been born poor and had no relative in a good position. He possessed a few small resources, deriving from his trade as a lawyer, which he practiced with his friend Luís Alves.

One night he had attended a performance of Otello, clapping until he split his gloves, acclaiming until his voice was tired, but ending the night satisfied with his fellows and with himself. When the performance was over he went, as was his custom, to watch the ladies leave, a procession of lace, and silk, and fans, and veils, and diamonds, and eyes of every color and language. Estêvão was punctual on these occasions of waiting, and seldom failed to be the last to leave. He now had his eyes fastened upon other eyes, not brown like his own, but blue, an iron blue, unfortunately married eyes, when he felt someone tap him on the shoulder and say these words softly:

"Let go of the chick, it belongs to the souls."

Estêvão turned around.

"Ah! it is you!" he said on seeing Luís Alves. "When did you arrive?"

"Today," answered his colleague. "I come thirsting for music. Vassouras has neither Lagrua nor Otello..."

"You came to wash your soul of the dust of the road," said Estêvão, who, even when speaking in prose, cultivated his poetic metaphors. "You did well; I would not have forgiven you if you had preferred the other, that gadabout whom they want to foist on us here as something grand, and who does not come up to the heels of the down..."

He broke off. Luís Alves had that instant ceremoniously greeted someone who was passing; Estêvão turned his head to see who it was. It was a young lady, whom he did not manage to see, because she was already going down the stairs; but so elegant and graceful that his eyes flashed with admiration.

"Some courtship?" he asked his friend.

"No; a neighbor."

The procession ended; the two left and went from there to sup at a hotel, then on to Botafogo, where Luís Alves had lived since losing his mother some months before.

Luís Alves's house stood almost at the end of the Praia de Botafogo, with another house on its right side, much larger and of a rich appearance. The night was fine, like the finest nights of that suburb. There was moonlight, a limpid sky, an infinity of stars and the wave beating softly on the beach, all the material, in short, of a good poetic composition, in twenty stanzas at least, bound to rich rhyme, with a few proparoxytones fished from the dictionaries. Estêvão made poetry, but he made it in prose, with a legitimate and sincere enthusiasm. Luís Alves, less inclined to beautiful things, preferred the most useful of all on that occasion, which was to go to sleep. He did not manage it without hearing from his guest everything he thought about that "chick that belonged to the souls," those blue eyes, "deep as the sky," Estêvão exclaimed.

At last they both slept; but whether because those eyes pursued him even in his dreams, or because he found the bed strange, or because destiny had so resolved, the truth is that Estêvão slept little, and, a rare thing, woke soon after daybreak appeared.

The morning was cool and serene; all was silence, barely broken by the beating of the sea and the chirping of little birds in the neighboring gardens. Estêvão, sulky at being unable to find sleep again, resolved to go and see the morning from closer by. He rose gently, washed, dressed, and asked for coffee to be brought to him in the garden, where he went with a book under his arm that he happened to find by the bed.

The garden lay at the back of the house; it was separated from the neighboring grounds by a fence. Glancing over those grounds, Estêvão saw that they were planted with care and art, quite vast, cut through by many curving paths and two great straight avenues. One of these began at the stone steps of the house and went to the end of the grounds; the other ran from Luís Alves's fence to the opposite extremity, cutting the first at the center. From where Estêvão stood only the second avenue could be seen from end to end.

The bachelor sat down on a bench he found there, received the cup of coffee that the slave brought him shortly after, lit a cigar and opened the book. The book was a Forensic Practice. Let us grant that he was right, given the spite with which he shut it and flung it to the ground, contenting himself with the song of the birds and the scent of the flowers, and his imagination too, which was worth the flowers and the birds.

God knows how far it would have gone, with the easy wings it had, had an incident not clipped them and brought it down to earth. From the neighboring house a dressing gown had come out (he saw nothing but a dressing gown) and had taken the avenue that faced the house, at a slow and meditative pace. Estêvão, who adored all dressing gowns, meditative or not, gave thanks to Providence for the good fortune that fell to him, and sharpened his eyes to contemplate that graceful early riser. Graceful, he did not yet know whether she was; but he decided she must be, precisely because he wished her to be.

The delightful landscape was at last to have a soul; the human element came to crown nature. Estêvão rose, to the whole of his tall and gentle stature, to see better (and to be seen, let us tell the whole truth) that unknown neighbor, who must surely be the one Luís Alves had greeted at the theater. A Christian and modest Actaeon, he did not surprise Diana in her bath, but on leaving it; nonetheless, he throbbed no less with emotion and curiosity.

The dressing gown went on walking.
