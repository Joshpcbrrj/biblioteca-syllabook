# 19. Conclusion

The wedding having been set for two months later, the whole interval was spent by the betrothed in that delightful way of living which is no longer the furtive colloquy of simple courtship, nor yet conjugal intimacy, but an intermediate and permitted state in which hearts may pour themselves freely into one another. Theirs had nothing of Estêvão's ecstatic and romantic love, but they loved sincerely, she even more than he, and the one as happy as the other.

The people who knew them commented on that unexpected affair in every manner and fashion, and more than one was gnawed by envy at the favor with which heaven had treated Luís Alves. The girl's grace and elegance met with no objection in anyone's mind; all confessed and applauded them, for even the mortified silence of some rival beauties, if there happened to be any, was also applause, and of the best. As to Guiomar's character, opinions differed greatly; and one day, when Luís Alves was telling her some snatches of conversation overheard by stealth, of which his betrothed was the subject, she seemed to reflect for a long time, and at last answered:

"It is no wonder there are so many different opinions; it is natural, for I have never made my mind common. However, the opinion of others matters little to me; I should like to know yours."

"Mine is that you are an angel."

Guiomar made a graceful gesture of annoyance, like one who had not expected that old and common compliment, yet eternally new, since there is none more ready and more beautiful in our Christian tongues. Her betrothed smiled, but said nothing to her, and yet he might have said something, that at least which the reader heard him say in one of the earlier chapters.

"If you do not know what I am," continued Guiomar, "I shall tell you myself, so that you may not marry me thus, by ambush, and that it may not befall you to unite yourself with a demon, supposing her an angel."

"A demon!" exclaimed Luís Alves, laughing.

"Neither more nor less," she retorted, laughing too. "Know, then, that I am very much mistress of my will, but little fond of expressing it; I want to be guessed and obeyed; I am also a little haughty, at times capricious, and on top of all this I have an exacting heart. See if it is possible to find so many faults together."

Luís Alves replied that these were all excellent qualities, and was on the point of saying that she had yet to mention another, which was the fundamental one of all; he preferred to allude to it after the wedding.

The wedding took place on the appointed day, with the customary solemnities. The morning of that day wore a mantle of dense mist, which our winter had put on her shoulders as if to shield her from the benign rigor of the temperature, a mantle which she shook off in a little while, in order to show herself as she was, a delicious and fresh Rio morning. Before long the sun struck full upon the calm blue waters, and upon those hills where the natural green alternated with the whiteness of human dwellings. No wind; only a breeze, soft and cool, which seemed the last breathing of the night now far away, and which only by fits stirred the leaves of the trees.

The grounds on that day were the same as on others, but Guiomar found in them a new and better aspect, a kind of divine expansion that animated the things around her. Every happy soul is pantheist; it seems to it that God smiles on it from within the blossoming flower, from the depths of the water that winds murmuring along, and even from amid the humble, rustic creeper, or in the rough and despised pebble on the ground. Such was Guiomar's soul that morning. Never had the trees, the flowers, the creeping grass seemed to her more luxuriant; the inner feeling drew in that outer life, just as the lung drank the pure morning air.

Mingled with these sensations common to every soul, there were those that were her own: hers, who saw there her last sun as an unmarried girl and contemplated in anticipation the new dawn, the long and happy day of her fervent ambitions. At this point her fancy shed the wings of wild leaf with which it had been hovering amid that vegetation, to don others of silk and brocade, and to fly God knows to what places of human grandeur.

Chance willed that on that morning she should wear the same dressing gown in which Estêvão had seen her from the other side of the fence, and wear on her bosom and at her wrists the same brooch and the same sapphire buttons. She did not have the book; but, in place of that circumstance, there was another, which was the same as on that famous morning: there were eyes on the other side of the fence watching her, in love. They were not, however, the same; they were her betrothed's, whom she went to meet with her own; and the most painful thing of all is that neither the fence nor the other accessories reminded her at all of the other man who was dying for her. Happiness is just this; it seldom has memory to spare for the sorrows of others.

The baroness seemed no less cheerful than she that day. Now and then the idea of her nephew rose in her memory, but there was no longer any sadness at not having brought about the marriage as she had wished; so light had the blow been to Jorge, and so indifferent did he go about, that the good lady understood that the love, if it had existed, was not great, and above all did not last; the thought that the same thing might have happened to him after six weeks of marriage made her give thanks to God for the failure of her plans.

Mrs. Oswald likewise showed herself happy, perhaps even more so, because she was so ostentatiously, as if she wished to redeem her past faults. Guiomar understood the latent intention of the noisy demonstrations with which she went about congratulating her; but the day was not one for rancor or resentment, and she received the Englishwoman's courtly flatteries with a smile.

The wedding was held at last. The tears the baroness shed when she saw Guiomar bound forever were the loveliest jewels she could give her. No mother shed them more sincerely; and, be it said to Guiomar's honor, no daughter received them more deeply into her heart.

On the night of the wedding, anyone who looked toward the sea would have seen, not far from the groups of curious onlookers drawn by the festivity of a large and wealthy house, the figure of a man seated on a flagstone that chance had left there. Whoever is accustomed to reading novels, and has read this narrative from the beginning, supposes at once that this man might be Estêvão. It was he. Perhaps the reader, in an identical predicament, would have taken refuge in a place so remote that the memory of the past could scarcely follow him. Estêvão's soul felt a cruel and singular need, the taste for turning the iron in the wound, something we shall call the voluptuousness of pain, for lack of a better name. And he went there to contemplate, with the indifferent and idle, that house where enjoyment and life reigned, at an hour that sank for him the past and the future on which he had lived. The constancy of the Stoic did not hold him; down his thin, pale face ran the last tears, and his heart, gathering what strength remained to it, beat hard within his breast.

Before him the fortunate mansion blazed with all its lights; behind him the wave beat slow and melancholy, and the depths of the cove could be seen, dark and sad. This arrangement of the place served the plan he had conceived, which was nothing less than to kill himself there and then, when he could no longer bear the pain, a kind of last revenge he wished to take on those who made him suffer so much, complicating their happiness with a remorse.

But this plan could not be carried out, for the reason that it was one more daydream, which dissipated like the others. Weakness of spirit denied him this last ambition. His eyes could gaze on death, as they could face fortune; but he lacked the means to walk toward it.

He stayed there, then, to the end; and instead of plunging into the water and into nothingness, as he had outlined, he went back home sadly, tottering like a drunkard, leaving there all his youth, for what he carried away was a thing colorless and dry, sterile and dead. The years passed afterward, and as they came, Estêvão went sinking into the vast, dark sea of the anonymous multitude. His name, which had never gone beyond the memory of his friends, died even there, when fortune took him far from them. Whether he still vegetates in some corner of the capital, or ended in some village of the interior, is not known.

Destiny was not to lie, and did not lie, to the ambition of Luís Alves. Guiomar had been right; he was the strong man. A month after they were married, as they were chatting of what newlyweds chat of, which is themselves, and recalling the short campaign of their courtship, Guiomar confessed to her husband that on that occasion she had recognized the whole power of his will.

"I saw that you were a resolute man," the girl said to Luís Alves, who, seated, was listening to her.

"Resolute and ambitious," added Luís Alves, smiling; "you must have perceived that I am both."

"Ambition is not a defect."

"On the contrary, it is a virtue; I feel that I have it, and that I shall make it prevail. I do not rely only on youth and moral force; I rely also on you, who will be a new force for me."

"Oh, yes!" exclaimed Guiomar.

And with a graceful manner she went on:

"But what do you give me in payment? A seat in the chamber? A minister's portfolio?"

"The luster of my name," he answered.

Guiomar, who was standing before him, her hands held in his, let herself fall slowly upon her husband's knees, and the two ambitions exchanged the fraternal kiss.

They fitted each other, as if that glove had been made for that hand.
