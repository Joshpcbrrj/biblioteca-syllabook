# 7. A Rival

It was not the first time Mrs. Oswald had alluded to something that displeased Guiomar, nor the first time the girl had answered her with the dryness the reader saw at the end of the previous chapter. The good Englishwoman remained serious and silent for some two or three minutes, looking at Guiomar, seemingly trying to question her thoughts, but in reality not knowing how to get out of the situation. The girl broke the silence:

"Very well," she said, smiling, "I see no reason for you to be angry with me."

"I am not angry," Mrs. Oswald replied promptly. "Angry, why? It grieves me, certainly, that nature does not grant me my way, and that an alliance so suitable for both is rejected by you; but if that is a cause for sorrow, it cannot be one for anger..."

"Sorrow?"

"For me... and naturally for him."

Guiomar answered with a simple shrug of the shoulders, dry and quick, like one who did not care about the harm or did not believe in it. Mrs. Oswald could not make out which of these impressions it was, and concluded it was both. The girl, however, seemed to regret that movement; she took the Englishwoman's hands, and with a voice even sweeter and softer than usual, said to her:

"See what it is to be a child! Does it not seem that on top of everything I am angry with you?"

"It does."

"Well, it is not so. These are the caprices of an ill-bred girl. I have taken to disliking it when people adore me... I lie; that I like; but I would wish to be only adored, do you not think so?"

And Guiomar accompanied these words with a pretty little laugh and the gestures of a mischievous child, which were entirely out of keeping with her habitual gravity.

"I understand: you like an adoration such as Dr. Estêvão's, silent and resigned, an adoration..."

And Mrs. Oswald, who, being a good Protestant, had Scripture at her fingertips, went on in this manner, stressing her words:

"An adoration such as Joseph, son of Jacob, must have inspired, who was beautiful like you: 'for him the maidens walked over the fence'..."

"The fence?" asked Guiomar, becoming serious.

"The wall, says Scripture, but I say the fence because... I do not know why myself. Do not blush! Take care, you are betraying yourself."

Guiomar had indeed blushed; but it was haughtiness and wounded pride that spoke in her face. She looked coldly and long at the Englishwoman, with one of those looks which are, so to speak, a gesture of the indignant soul. What irritated her was not the allusion, which was not worth much; it was the person who made it, an inferior and a hireling. Mrs. Oswald perceived as much; she bit the tip of her lip, but gave way to the girl.

"My God!" she said. "It seems you have taken offense at an idle joke. You know very well I could not wish to aggrieve you; to suppose it is to offend me, me, who also bear you a mother's affection..."

The last word calmed Guiomar's spirit; she had yielded to the impulse of her haughty character, but reason came afterward, and the heart too, which was not a bad one. The Englishwoman, who had long practice of life and knew how to yield in time, joined gesture to word and drew her to her with her arms. Guiomar let herself go, a little unwillingly, and the conversation would have ended there, had Mrs. Oswald not said to her, in the sweetest voice that could come from that throat:

"Be convinced that I am importunate and indiscreet out of affection, and that the happiness of this family is the whole ambition of my soul. There can be no better intention than this. One last piece of advice, last if you do not allow me to speak to you of it again: I believe you dream, perhaps, too much. Are you dreaming of some storybook love, all but impossible? I tell you that you do wrong, that it is better, much better, to be content with reality; if it is not brilliant like dreams, it has at least the advantage of existing."

Guiomar fixed her eyes on the floor this time, with the vague, lifeless look of one whose mind has gone dark to outward things. Did Mrs. Oswald's words perhaps answer some inner voice of hers? The Englishwoman went on in the same train of ideas, without her interrupting or giving any sign of herself. When she finished, Guiomar started, as if waking; she raised her head and, slowly and moved, gave this single reply:

"Perhaps you are right, Mrs. Oswald, but in any case dreams are so good!"

Mrs. Oswald shook her head and went out; Guiomar followed her with her eyes, smiling, satisfied with herself, and murmuring so low that her own heart scarcely heard her:

"Dreams, no; pure reality."

I suppose the reader is curious to know who the happy or unhappy mortal was, of whom the two had spoken in the preceding dialogue, if he has not already suspected that it was none other than the baroness's nephew, that young man whom I pointed out to him in passing on the stairs of the Ginásio.

He was a young man of twenty-five or twenty-six. Jorge was his name; he was not ugly, but art spoiled a little the work of nature. Too much pampering harms the plant, said the poet, and that maxim is applicable not only to poetry, but also to man. Jorge had a handsome chestnut mustache, pomaded and stiffened with excessive care. His eyes, clear and lively, would have been finer if he had not moved them with an affectation that was at times feminine. I will say the same of his manners, which would have been easy and natural had he not made them so trim and measured. His words came out slow and counted, as if to make one feel all the munificence of the author. He did not utter them like other people; each syllable was, so to speak, squeezed out, and it was easy to see after a few minutes that he made the whole beauty of elocution consist in that lengthening of the word. His ideas were on a par with his manner of expressing them; they were hollow inside, but wore a crust of ponderous gravity, which made one want to go and refresh one's ear with light and merry things.

Such were Jorge's apparent defects. There were others, and of these the greatest was a mortal sin, the seventh. The name his father had left him, and his aunt's influence, might have served him in making a career in some public post; he preferred, however, to vegetate idly, living on the savings he had inherited from his parents and on the hopes he had in the baroness's affection. No other occupation was known of him.

Notwithstanding the defects mentioned, there were good qualities in him; he knew how to devote himself, he was generous, incapable of doing harm, and had a sincere love for his old relative. The baroness, for her part, was very fond of him. Guiomar and he were her two chief affections, almost her only ones.

Such was the person whose interests Mrs. Oswald defended, out of love for the baroness, and no less for her own sake. The baroness too had her dreams, as she herself said, and these were to make those two children happy. Jorge for his part was ready to offer his neck to the sacrifice; and, when all was well considered, perhaps he sincerely loved the girl. The difference between him and Estêvão was that his love was as measured as his gestures, and as superficial as his other impressions.

From what has been said here, the reader will easily understand that, of the two suitors, only one at once perceived the feeling of the other. Estêvão's soul lived in his eyes, filling them so that he could see nothing else beyond Guiomar.

At the end of two weeks Estêvão's situation could be called less bad; in his opinion it was excellent. The baroness learned who he was; Guiomar had told her everything; but the Englishwoman, no less than her own observation, showed her that Guiomar ran no danger, and with the danger excluded, there remained the bachelor's good qualities, which quite won her favor. Mrs. Oswald sailed in the same placid waters. Jorge himself, naturally because he trusted in himself, did not fear the rival, and before long opened to him the gates of his gravity. What wonder, then, that Guiomar herself relaxed a little her first rigidity?

That good young man had the salutary credulity of hope, in which so often all the blessings of life are summed up. He asked for much, being a thirsty soul, but very little sufficed to content him. His imagination multiplied the zeros; with a grain of sand he would have built a world. The affability of some and the courtesy of others were enough for him to judge himself almost at the end of his aspirations; and although Guiomar gave him not one of the encouragements of other days, which were, besides, so frail, he nevertheless believed devoutly that love was being born, or reborn, in that rebellious heart.

Guiomar, amid the affections that surrounded her, knew how to keep herself above the hopes of some and the suspicions of others. Equally courteous, but equally impassive to all, she moved her eyes with the serenity of detachment, not enamored, nor even coquettish. She would have had, had she wished, the art of Armida; she would have known how to curb or goad hearts, according as they were impatient or tepid; but she lacked the taste, or rather, she had an excess of the feeling of what she took to be her personal dignity.
