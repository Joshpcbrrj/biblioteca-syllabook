# 13. Explanations

Luís Alves had understood the whole expression of Guiomar's eyes; but he was a cold, resolute man. He inclined his torso with every correct and well-bred grace, and said to her in the mildest voice that his strong and severe organ allowed:

"It seems to you that I was a little bold, does it not? I was merely sincere; and though your delicacy may condemn me, I am sure that there is mercy enough in your heart..."

Guiomar had regained full possession of herself.

"You are mistaken," she said; "I do not condemn you, for the simple reason that I did not understand you."

"So much the better," retorted Luís Alves without blinking; "my offense in that case did not go beyond the sphere of intention."

"But... were you referring to the journey?"

"I was; I was asking when you were leaving."

This presence of mind in Luís Alves went very well with Guiomar's temper; it was a bond of sympathy. The girl answered that the commander would come to fetch them in a fortnight or three weeks.

"Only three months?" asked the lawyer.

"Three or four."

"Four months is not an eternity, but Cantagalo, for a carioca born and bred, must be an exile, or nearly so... Let us hope," continued Luís Alves, concluding sooner than he wished on seeing Jorge approach the window, "let us hope that this exile will not make you forget what I solemnly tell you at this moment: that you have a great and noble soul, and that I admire it!"

Jorge had arrived; the conversation had to end or take a different course.

Luís Alves's last words were singularly fitted to leave a deep furrow in the girl's memory. It was not a declaration of love, nor a drawing-room courtesy, things she had heard many a time, which could flatter her, and certainly did flatter her; it was more than a compliment and did not come to be a declaration. There was no emotion in the lawyer's voice; firmness, yes, and an air of deep conviction. Guiomar looked at him almost without noticing Jorge's presence; but Luís Alves had turned to the newcomer and was speaking to him in a jovial tone, quite different from the one he had used a little before.

If this contrast was premeditated (I do not know whether it was), it could not have suited Guiomar's spirit better. Of all the men the girl had known till then, he was the first who inspired curiosity in her, and also, on that occasion, the first person who took pity on her. Consider, reader: curiosity and gratitude; see whether there are two wings better suited to hurl one soul into the bosom of another soul, or into an abyss, which is sometimes the same thing.

I said "took pity," and this word alone, unaccompanied by anything else, may lead the reader to believe that during those days in which we lost sight of her Guiomar had become an unhappy creature. Nothing of the kind; the situation was the same, not the same as before Jorge's letter, but the same as on the night she received it, a situation, certainly, sufficiently somber and heavy for a heart that fears being constrained, but neither desperate nor anguished.

The baroness, if she had known the facts, or if she had been able to read the girl's soul, would have been the first to give her every consolation. But she did not know. Her desire, or rather the dream of her old age, as she said in one of the earlier chapters, was to leave her goddaughter and her nephew happy, and she held that the best means of leaving them happy was to marry them to each other. The information she had of the girl's heart in this respect was incomplete or inexact; they painted as coldness what was repugnance. Mrs. Oswald always gave her hopes of a happy and near success; the girl's angers she never told her. Of Jorge's letter she knew nothing, nor of the scene that took place in the bedchamber. The marriage continued to appear to her with all the probabilities of an attainable hope.

The lady reader will say that the nephew did not deserve so much zeal nor so tenacious a hope, and she will be right; but the baroness's eyes are not the lady reader's; she saw only his good side, which was really good, though with a relative goodness; but she did not see the bad side, she did not see nor could she see the grave frivolity of his mind, nor the kind of affection that was being born in his heart.

Jorge was her only blood relation, the son of a sister who had lived unhappy and died still more unhappily, not repudiated, but loathed by her husband, a circumstance that made the young man dear to her. More than her goddaughter, no; not so much, certainly; the heart could not divide itself equally into such great portions; but she was very fond of him, enough to wish him happy, and to work to make him so. Let us add that her sister's fate was always present in her mind, and that she feared a like lot for Guiomar; in Jorge she seemed to see all the gifts needed to make her fortunate.

Unfortunately, Mrs. Oswald, knowing of those secret wishes and more or less the confidante of Jorge's feelings, found this a favorable occasion to display all the gratitude with which she was possessed and the deep friendship that bound her to the baroness's family. To interpose herself in order to serve others, and still more herself. She saw the difficulty, but did not lose heart; it was necessary to lay a snare for the baroness's gratitude. So she did not hesitate to confide to Guiomar her godmother's wish, exaggerating it, however, because the baroness had never said that "such a marriage was her campaign," and Mrs. Oswald attributed to her this phrase, deadly to all the girl's hopes and dreams. But if she spoke too much to the one, she was much more sparing of words with the other, and from the exaggeration or the attenuation of the truth there had resulted that perennial state of stifled struggle, of fears, of indecision and of secret bitterness. It should be said, to give the last stroke to the portrait, that this Mrs. Oswald followed not only the voice of her personal interest, but also the impulse of her own genius, fond of putting her natural sagacity to the test, of attempting and carrying through one of those delicate and difficult operations, so that, if there were such a thing as domestic diplomacy, or if posts were created for it, Mrs. Oswald could count on a place as ambassadress.

Coming now to the narration of the events of the story, the reader must know that Jorge's letter received no reply, written or verbal. The day after its delivery he went to dine at Botafogo; but Guiomar had not left her room, on the pretext of a headache; the baroness spent the day with her; Jorge managed only to learn, when he left, that the girl was better. On the following days no reply came into the suitor's hands, nor did he manage to obtain some five minutes of solitary conversation with the girl; Guiomar always slipped away, with that supreme art of the woman who loathes, which is neither more nor less than that of the woman who loves.

One day, however, there was no way of escape; and Jorge, who had no emotion in his voice, because he had little in his heart, looked at her with straight eyes and frankly asked her for a word of hope or of disenchantment. The girl hesitated a few seconds; yet she had to answer. She overcame her repugnance, saying to him with a cold smile:

"Neither one thing nor the other."

"Not even disenchantment?" asked Jorge, flustered.

"No one can give either one thing or the other," she said; "we are used to accepting them from our destiny."

It was not an answer, as the reader sees; Jorge was about to ask for a more transparent decision, but the girl had taken advantage of the first impression and slipped away. When he recovered his voice he saw no more than the hem of her dress, vanishing round the turn of a door.

Guiomar shortened the reins on the familiarity that existed between her and Jorge; but if she treated him with more reserve, she did not do so with dryness or coldness, nor did she cease to be polite and affable. The natural dignity that was in her whole person served her, besides, as a sort of ivory tower, in which she shut herself up as in a castle and kept her suitor at a respectful distance.

Of the two men who wanted her, neither spoke to her soul; she felt that Estêvão belonged to the phalanx of the lukewarm, Jorge to the tribe of the incapable, two classes of men who had no elective affinity with her. She did not, certainly, rate the two suitors equally; one was simply trivial, the other merely sentimental; but neither of them capable of creating his destiny by himself. If she did not rate them equally, neither did she see them with the same eyes; Jorge bored her, he was a Diogenes of a new species; through the tattered cloak of his importance one saw his sad vulgarity throbbing. Estêvão inspired somewhat more respect in her; he was an ardent and slack soul, born to desire, not to conquer, a kind of condor, able to stare at the sun but without wings to fly up to it. Guiomar's feeling toward Estêvão could never come to love; it had much of superiority and forgiveness.

With another temperament, different aspirations, and living in a different sphere, she would certainly have loved him, as he loved her. But nature and society joined hands to turn her aside from purely intimate pleasures. She asked for love, but would not have wished to enjoy it in an obscure life; the greatest happiness on earth would have been for her the greatest of misfortunes, if it were set down for her in a wilderness. As a child, her eyes had gone after the silks and the jewels of the women she saw in the estate adjoining her mother's poor yard; as a young woman, they went in the same way after the brilliant spectacle of social grandeur. She wanted a man who, alongside a youthful heart capable of loving, should feel within himself strength enough to raise her to where all eyes would see her. Voluntarily, only once had she accepted obscurity and mediocrity: it was when she proposed to follow the trade of teaching; but it must be said that she counted on the baroness's tenderness.
