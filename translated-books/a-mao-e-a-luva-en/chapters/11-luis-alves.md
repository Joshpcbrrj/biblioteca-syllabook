# 11. Luís Alves

For one whole long week Estêvão failed to appear at the office where he worked with Luís Alves; nor did he appear in Botafogo. No one saw him in all that time in the places where he was more or less a regular. It was six days, I do not say of absolute seclusion, but of complete solitude, for even the few times he went out, he always did so at hours or in directions where he saw no one and no one saw him.

But had it not been for this raw and ill-starred crisis, it is almost certain that he would have set a lance in the Africa of those days, which was a very serious and weighty matter, the great question of the Rua do Ouvidor and of José Tomás's house, the ponderous, thorny and complicated question of whether Stephanoni would make her debut in Ernani. This question, at which the reader laughs today, as his nephews will laugh at other, similar puerilities, this pretension, which Lagrua opposed on the grounds that Ernani was hers, a pretension that made souls and printing presses groan in those days, was a thing most apt to rouse the mettle of our Estêvão, who was as much a marshal in small matters as a raw recruit in great ones.

Unfortunately he did not appear, he did not even know of the conflict and the debate, occupied as he was in waging the harsh and bloody duel of man against himself, when the support or the consolation of other men fails him. He was all Guiomar; Guiomar was the first and the last thought of every day. The girl's shadow lived beside him and within him, in the book he read, in the solitary street where he happened to pass, in the dreams of the night, in the stars of the sky, in the few flowers of his untended garden.

A perspicacious reader, as I suppose the reader of this book must be, will excuse me from relating the many plans he wove, diverse and contradictory, as is only reasonable in similar situations. I shall say merely, in passing, that he thought three times of dying, twice of fleeing the city, four times of going to drown his mortal grief in that still more mortal swamp of corruption in which the flower of youth so often rots and dies. In all this his mind was but the plaything of continual and varied sensations. The strength, the permanence of his affection was not enough to give sequence and reality to the vague conceptions of his brain, a brain sickly even when he was in health.

The idea of suicide lodged itself deeper in his mind one afternoon when he went out to clear his head and saw a funeral passing on its way to Caju. The procession was sad, made still sadder by the indifference to be read on the faces of those who piously accompanied the dead man. Estêvão uncovered his head and sincerely wished to be in there, shut inside those narrow pine boards, with all his sorrows, passions and hopes.

"I have no other recourse," he thought; "I must die. It is a single pain, and it is freedom."

On his way home, a child playing in the street, shirtless, with his feet in the muddy water of the gutter, made him stop for a few moments, envious of that good fortune of childhood, which laughs with its feet in the puddle. But the envy of death and the envy of innocence were in turn replaced by the envy of happiness, when on returning he saw the windows of a neighboring house open, and the parlor lit up, and a bride crowned with orange blossoms, smiling at the bridegroom, who smiled equally at her, both with the indefinable and unique smile of the occasion.

The five days passed in this way, bound up with disgust, despair, tears, bitter reflections, useless sighs, until the dawn of the sixth day broke, and with it, or a little after it, a letter from Botafogo. When Estêvão saw the baroness's servant at the door of the room with a letter in his hand, he felt such a flutter that he heard nothing of what the man said to him.

Did he suppose the letter was from Guiomar? Perhaps; but the illusion lasted only the few moments he took to break the seal and unfold the sheet of paper inside.

The letter was from the baroness.

The baroness asked him graciously whether he had died, and begged him to come and speak to her about the lawsuit she was carrying on. Estêvão had already reached the state of waiting only for a pretext to come to terms with himself; none could have been better. He wrote two lines of reply quickly, and at one o'clock in the afternoon he stepped down from a tilbury at the door of the fatal and delightful house where he had spent the best and the worst hours of his life.

"Do you know why I have put you to this trouble, besides the pleasure I take in seeing you?" the baroness asked immediately after the first greetings.

"You told me it was about the lawsuit..."

"Yes, we must settle a few things before our departure."

"Are you leaving the Court, madam?"

"We are going to the country."

Estêvão turned pale. In his situation that journey was the best thing that could happen to him; nevertheless the news hurt him. The conversation that followed was wholly on the legal matter, and lasted a long hour, without Guiomar's appearing. On taking his leave, Estêvão ventured to ask after her.

"She is out walking," answered the baroness.

Estêvão took leave of his client, who accompanied him as far as the door of the room, repeating some recommendations to him, which the lawyer scarcely heard and which absolutely did not stay in his memory.

The hope of seeing the girl had brought him to that house more than anything else; he was leaving without the pleasure of gazing on her once more; more than that, threatened with not seeing her again soon, or who knows whether ever. He was reflecting on this and nearing the door, where a carriage was stopping at the same moment. Estêvão naturally started, before seeing who was to alight; he clung to the doorway, his eyes fixed on the carriage door, which a footman opened in haste.

The first figure to descend was our acquaintance Mrs. Oswald, who did so without giving Estêvão time to offer her his hand. The bachelor, from the moment he had seen her, had quickly approached the carriage door.

Guiomar descended soon after. The hand clasped in a pearl-colored glove rested lightly on Estêvão's hand, which trembled all over. The girl gave him a smiling greeting, murmured a word of thanks and withdrew with the Englishwoman. It was little; but that little stirred the bachelor, who made off from there for the city, in the direction of the office.

Luís Alves was surprised to see him; not with a six-day surprise, as he ought to have been, but a forty-eight-hour one at most. And no wonder. Luís Alves's preoccupation in those days was his electoral candidacy; the good news was due to reach him by the next mail from the North. Now, in all reason, a man who is about to be inscribed on the tablets of parliament cannot give much thought to the loves of a young man, even if the young man is a friend and the loves are true.

Estêvão wasted no time on circumlocutions; he walked in and poured out his whole soul, distressed and consoled at once, on the breast of his old friend and companion. To each passage of the full confession he made there, Luís Alves answered with a comment, now serious, now playful. But when Estêvão gave him the news that the baroness's family was going to the country, Luís Alves withdrew the half-smile that had rested on his lips from the start, and with the most sudden and sincere astonishment exclaimed:

"To the country!"

"The baroness said so just now."

"But..."

Luís Alves did not finish; he looked, still half doubtful, at Estêvão, and remained silent for some time, scratching his chin with the ivory knife and looking at an engraving that hung on the wall opposite.

"In the situation I am in," Estêvão went on, "you will say that the journey is a piece of luck for me. Well, it is not; I will not allow the journey. If she leaves the Court, I leave too."

"You are mad!"

"Perhaps."

Luís Alves emerged from that natural indifference with which he always listened to him and spoke to him on such a subject. He spoke to him tenderly, perhaps for the first time in his life. What he told him was merely an enlarged edition of what he had told him on earlier occasions, now with greater foundation, because after Guiomar's formal disenchantment there was no other recourse but to go and forget her entirely.

"Oh! never that!" Estêvão interrupted. "Besides, I do not know, I am not sure whether she spoke from the heart that afternoon..."

The candor with which Estêvão said this was the faithful translation of his mind, and the reason for such words the reader need seek nowhere but in that smile of a little while before, beside the carriage, a smile that danced in his brain like a ray of sun filtered through the black clouds of a storm.

Luís Alves shook his head and ran his eyes over the scribbled pages of some case papers he had before him, and began to leaf through them slowly. Suddenly he gave a little pat, with his flat hand, upon the papers, and raised his head:

"There is perhaps a way of finding out everything," he said, "of finding out whether she truly loves you, or... I can try it, on one condition."

"What?"

"The condition that you give up your pretensions. What the devil do you gain by nursing a passion that has neither efficacy nor remedy?"

This promise was the hardest that could be wrung from a heart in which generations of hopes succeeded one another almost without a break; Estêvão nevertheless made it, perhaps with the secret resolution of betraying it.

Luís Alves was left alone a few minutes later. The last words he said to his colleague were two or three young man's jests; but as soon as he was alone he became serious, and leaning his body forward, with his arms on the desk, scraping his nails with a penknife, he remained there a long while as if reflecting, far from Estêvão, who in any case was no longer near, and still farther from the case papers before him. But what was he thinking of, if not of Estêvão, nor of the papers, nor, for now, of his electoral hopes? Patience, reader; you shall know presently. Content yourself with the news that, at the end of twenty minutes of that abstraction, Luís Alves came to himself, uttering aloud this simple remark:

"There is no doubt; she is an ambitious woman."

And freed from that preoccupation, he buried himself wholly in the reading of the papers.
