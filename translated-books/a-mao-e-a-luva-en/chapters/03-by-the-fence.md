# 3. By the Fence

The first thing Estêvão could discover was that the neighbor was young. He saw her profile, in each opening the trees left, a correct and pure profile, like an ancient sculpture. He saw her milk-white face, against which stood out the dark color of her hair, not combed once and for all but loosely tied on top of her head, with that morning carelessness which makes beautiful women more beautiful. The dressing gown, of white muslin, finely embroidered, did not let the whole grace of her figure be seen, which must have been, and was, elegant, with the elegance that is born with a creature or is refined by education, asking nothing, or little, of the seamstress's scissors. The whole bosom was covered up to the neck, where the gown was fastened by a small sapphire brooch. A button of the same mineral closed at each wrist the narrow, smooth sleeves, which ended in flounces of lace.

Estêvão, from the distance and in the position where he found himself, could not see all these minutiae which I set down for you here, in fulfillment of this duty of mine as a teller of tales. What he saw, besides the profile, the hair, and the white complexion, was the girl's stature, which was tall, perhaps a little less than it seemed in the trailing dress she wore. He could also see a little book, open in her hands, on which she rested her eyes, raising them from time to time, when she needed to turn the page, and letting them fall again to soak in her reading.

She went on walking thus, never thinking that anyone saw her, as serene and grave as if she were crossing a drawing room. Estêvão, who did not take his eyes off her, mentally begged heaven for the fortune of having her closer, and longed to see her reach the avenue that lay before him. Yet it was hard for her to seem lovelier than she was, seen thus in profile, escaping between the trees. The young bachelor, so as not to lose the habit of his first days, called up all his literary reminiscences; the unknown lady was successively compared to a seraph of Klopstock, to a fairy of Shakespeare, to all that in his memory was most airy, transparent, ideal.

While he busied his mind with these poetic comparisons, not out of place, if you like, in such a spot and beside so graceful a creature, she walked slowly on and had reached the crossing of the two great avenues of the grounds. Estêvão hoped she would turn to the right, that is, come toward his side, but above all he feared she would go straight on along the same avenue and be lost at the far end of the grounds. The girl chose a middle course, turned to the left, giving her back to her curious admirer and continuing at the same slow, regular pace.

The grounds were not excessively large; and however slow the early riser's step, she would not spend an immense time in walking to the end of that stretch of the avenue she had entered. But there, beside that youthful and impatient heart, each minute seemed, I shall not say a century, which would be to abuse the rights of style, but an hour, an hour it certainly seemed to him.

The girl meanwhile, on reaching the end, stopped a few moments and rested her hand on the back of a rustic bench that stood there facing another placed at the opposite extremity. Her other hand had dropped, and her eyes too, which pained her curious observer. Was it longing for someone? Estêvão felt something I shall call anticipated jealousy, but which in reality was envy of another's fortune. Envy is an evil feeling; but in him, who was born to love, and who, besides, bore within himself the contrast between his birth and his instinct, an obscure cradle and aspirations to an elegant life, in him envy was almost an excusable feeling.

The girl turned back and came up the avenue. At last, Estêvão said to himself, I shall contemplate her from closer by. At the same time, fearing that on discovering a stranger there she might turn her steps toward the house, Estêvão moved away from the place where he had stood, resolved to appear when she was near the fence of the garden. The girl came walking with the book closed, and her eyes now on the ground, now on the swallows and the camaxilras that fluttered about the grounds. If she carried a longing, it could not be read in her face, which was quiet and pensive, yes, but without the least shadow of sorrow or sadness.

From where he stood Estêvão could examine her features without being seen by her; but that was precisely what he did not think of, once he could make them out. It was worth the trouble, however, to contemplate those large brown eyes, half veiled by long, fine and thick lashes, neither tender nor languid, as he had fancied he saw them, but of a severe, chaste and cold beauty.

It was worth the trouble to admire how they communicated to the whole face and to the whole figure an air of tranquil majesty, mistress of itself. She was not one of those beauties that, while they subjugate the heart, kindle the senses; she spoke to the intelligence sooner than to the heart, so much did art seem to have collaborated with nature in that creature, half statue and half woman.

All this our bachelor could see and consider. The truth, however, is that he attended to none of these things. From the moment he had made out the girl's features he stood as if seized with astonishment, his eyes fixed, his mouth half open, life and all his blood fleeing to his heart.

The girl had reached the fence; she stood a while, looked around and at last sat on the bench there, with her back to Luís Alves's garden. She opened the book again, and went on reading from where she had left off, so alone with herself, so absorbed in the book before her, that she was not roused by the noise, in any case faint, of Estêvão's steps on the dry leaves of the ground. She might have read half a page when Estêvão, leaning over the fence, and trying to muffle his voice so that it should reach only her ears, uttered this simple name:

"Guiomar!"

The girl gave a cry of surprise and fright, and turned, startled, toward the side from which the voice had come. At the same time she had risen. The impression it made upon her, and I do not know whether also some air of anger he noticed in her face; and above all, remorse at not having stifled that cry of his heart, made Estêvão, almost in the same instant, murmur in a tone of entreaty:

"Forgive me; it was a spark of the past that lay under the ashes: it has gone out altogether."

Guiomar (we know now that this was her name) looked serious and still at her ill-fated interrupter, for two long and deadly minutes. Estêvão, confused and mortified, kept his eyes on the ground; his heart beat hard, as if taking leave of life. The situation was too distressing and embarrassing to be prolonged any further. Estêvão was about to bow and take his leave; but the girl, with a smile of more pity than affection, murmured:

"You are forgiven."

She walked to the fence and held out her hand, which he pressed (pressed is not quite right), which he barely touched, as ceremoniously as he could and ought to in that situation.

And then they stood looking at each other, neither daring to say anything, nor to go away, both seeing the specter of the past, that past so bitter for one of them. Guiomar was the first to break the silence, putting to Estêvão a natural question, as was inevitable in those circumstances, but still, or for that very reason, the harshest he could hear:

"It is two years since we last saw each other, I believe?"

"Two years," murmured Estêvão, stifling a sigh.

"You have graduated, have you not? I remember having read your name..."

"I have graduated. You know it was my aunt's greatest wish..."

"I have not seen her for a long time," Guiomar interrupted; "I left the school soon after you went to São Paulo. I left at the invitation of the baroness, my godmother, who came to fetch me there one day, alleging that I had nothing more to learn, and that it did not suit me to teach."

"Certainly," Estêvão assented. "My aunt could not but go on teaching; she ended in the profession."

"Ended?"

"She died."

"Ah!"

"She died about a year ago."

"She was a good creature," Guiomar went on, after some moments of silence, "very affectionate and very accomplished. I owe her what I learned... Are you admiring this flower?"

Estêvão, caught in flagrant delict of admiration, not of the flower but of the hand that held it (a delicious hand, which must surely be the one lost from the Venus de Milo), stammered:

"Indeed, it is lovely!"

"There are many pretty flowers here in the grounds. The baroness has an immense taste for these things, and our gardener is a man who knows his trade."

That natural shyness of the first occasion gradually disappeared, and the conversation came to be, not as familiar as of old, but in any case less cold than it had been at first. There was, however, a difference between the two: he, notwithstanding his ease, felt shaken and moved; she, on the other hand, having overcome her initial start, showed herself calm and cool, ever polite and grave, smiling at times, but with a smile on the surface of her face, which did not alter her serenity and composure.

The place and the hour were better suited to an idyll than to a cold and colorless talk. A clear and limpid sky, a pure air, the sun filtering a still faint and tepid light through the leaves, the vegetation all around, all that reviving of things seemed to be asking for a like dawn in souls. These should have spoken there their own language, loving and candid, instead of the other, courteous, elegant and stiff, which displeased neither of them, certainly, but which was much less voluntary on Estêvão's lips.

Guiomar spoke with a certain grace, somewhat stiff and measured, without vivacity or warmth. Estêvão, who for the most part had remained listening to her, observed to himself that the girl's manners were not unnatural to her, though they might be calculated for that situation. The Guiomar he had known and loved was the embryo of the Guiomar of today, the sketch of the canvas now perfect; she had lacked color then, but the lines of the drawing could already be seen.

The conversation lasted about three quarters of an hour, a crumb of time to him, who had wished for much more. But it had to end; she was the first to tell him so.

"You have made me lose a great deal of time. We have perhaps been here talking for an hour. It was natural, after two years. Two years! But what was not natural," she went on, changing her tone, "was for me to dare to speak to a stranger in this déshabillé, so little elegant..."

"Most elegant, on the contrary."

"You always have a compliment in reserve: I see you did not waste your time at the academy. I am going. It is time for the baroness to take her walk through the grounds."

"Is that the lady standing there at the top of the stairs?" asked Estêvão.

"The very one," answered Guiomar. "She is waiting for me to give her my arm."

And with a coldly aristocratic gesture, she held out her hand to Estêvão, saying:

"Farewell, Doctor; I was glad to see you."

Estêvão touched her hand lightly, fine and soft, and bowed respectfully.

The girl walked toward the house. He followed her with his eyes, admiring the grace with which she, this time at a quickened pace, glided among the trees until she mounted the steps of the house. He saw her give her arm to her godmother, and the two come down and go slowly along the same path by which Guiomar had gone the first time.

Estêvão remained a while longer leaning on the fence, in the hope that she would glance or turn her steps to that side; she, however, passed by indifferent, as if she did not know of his existence. Estêvão withdrew from there with bowed head and sad, beset by contrary feelings, full of a sadness and a joy that scarcely combined, and above it all the vague, muffled echo of this question:

"Am I entering a drama or leaving a comedy?"
