# 9. Conspiracy

The baroness, when the two speakers at the fence came up to her, grew more fearful and more perplexed. Guiomar came smiling and even jesting; but Estêvão's dejection was so ill disguised that it had to be one of two things: either she was delivering to him his final disenchantment, or it was merely a serious tiff, which the young man could not or would not hide from strange eyes. That is what the baroness thought. What she concluded was that, in any case, it was urgent to attempt something in favor of the greatest, the only dream of her old age.

Jorge did not perceive the true reason why his aunt had told him it was necessary to talk with her; he imagined it would be about Guiomar and Estêvão, but he was far from supposing the full scope of the interview.

The interview could not take place at once that day; the visitors stayed late, and the evening was the most pleasant and diverting of all evenings; Guiomar, above all, was as never before, merry and interesting. Serenity seemed to dwell in her soul and be reflected in her face, so often pensive, but now so cold and so bare.

It will not be necessary to tell a shrewd reader of good will... Oh! above all of good will, for one needs it, and a great deal of it, to come this far and follow to the end a story like this, in which the author is more occupied with drawing one or two characters and setting forth some human feelings than with anything else, because he would not venture to do anything else; it will not be necessary to declare to the reader, I was saying, that all that gaiety of Guiomar's was daggers plunged into our Estêvão's breast. He could not suppose her dejected; but grieved, at least, a little respectful toward the pain in him, that, yes, he imagined it would be. But it was nothing of the kind, and the poor young man left sooner than he had thought or wished to leave.

In her bedchamber, if he could have seen her later in her bedchamber, solitary and wholly to herself, seated in the low armchair beside the bed, her hair undone, her little feet in black satin slippers, her hands in her lap and her eyes wandering from object to object, as if reproducing outwardly the inward attitudes of thought, there not only would he have adored her on his knees, but he might even have supposed that some preoccupation robbed her of sleep, and that it was none other than himself.

Perhaps it was; in part at least it would be he. Guiomar did not have so bad a heart that the sorrows of a man who had been right or wrong to love her did not pain her. But whether one or many were the causes of that preoccupation, the truth is that it lasted a long time. Guiomar passed from the armchair to the window, which she opened wide, to contemplate the night: the moonlight striking the waters, the serene and eternal sky. Eternal, yes, eternal, my lady reader, which is the most disconsoling lesson God could give us, in the midst of our agitations, struggles, anxieties, insatiable passions, griefs of a day, joys of an instant, that end and pass away with us, beneath that blue eternity, impassive and mute as death.

Did Guiomar think of this? No, she did not think of it for a single minute; she belonged wholly to life and to the world, her heart was now unfolding, she lived in full dawn. What did it matter to her, or who had ever made her understand this dry and arid philosophy? She lived in the present and the future, and, so great was her future, I mean the ambitions that filled it, so great that it was enough to occupy her thought, even if the present had given her nothing more. Of the past she wished to know nothing; probably she had forgotten it.

Dawn found her sleeping; but the first rays of the sun came to wake her, as usual, for the morning walk with her godmother. Guiomar sacrificed everything to the filial devotion of which she had already given so many proofs. The baroness, however, was preoccupied; the walk was different from those of other days.

At noon Guiomar got into the carriage with Mrs. Oswald, and they went out on a visit. The baroness remained alone; Jorge did not let her stay alone for long, for he arrived a little later.

The baroness did not waste time in circumlocutions. As soon as she saw her nephew she questioned him directly:

"I was told, it was Mrs. Oswald who told me, that you are fond of Guiomar."

Jorge did not much count on such a question; yet he was not so ingenuous as to blush, nor so enamored that his voice should tremble. He gravely pulled down his shirt cuffs, straightened his cravat, and answered simply:

"I did not dare to speak to you of these things..."

"Why not?" interrupted the baroness; "they are subjects that can be treated between you and me without pain to either of us. Is it then true, what Mrs. Oswald told me?"

"It is."

"Do you love truly, or..."

"Truly. I would draw back if I saw that an alliance between us were unbecoming to the luster of our family; but although she is..."

"Guiomar is my daughter," the baroness hastened to say.

"Precisely; there can be no better title."

"She has another," the baroness continued; "she has an angelic and pure soul. Henriqueta had no better heart nor more love for her own. Besides, nature gave her a superior mind, so that fortune did no more than amend the mistake of birth. And finally she is of a beauty not common..."

"Rare, aunt, you may say she is of a rare beauty," put in Jorge, and for the first time something shone in his eyes that was not his usual gravity.

"You see, then," the baroness went on, "that she has every right to the love and the hand of a man such as you."

The baroness had an ingenuous and smooth heart, without deviations or cunning; yet there are occasions when the most upright spirit employs, as if by instinct, diplomatic subtleties. The good lady had so much at heart that union of her nephew with her goddaughter that she did not trust to love alone; she sought to interest his self-esteem as well.

Jorge bowed with affected modesty.

"A man such as I," he said, "is worth little in himself; the worth I have, and it is a great deal, comes from the name of my parents and of yours, aunt, and from the saintly qualities that adorn you..."

"Only one, Jorge, only one most saintly quality: that of loving you and her. That is why my pleasure was immense when Mrs. Oswald told me you were fond of Guiomar. Believe me, if I had the fortune to see you two united and happy, I would die content."

"Oh! that!" said Jorge with an air of doubt.

"Do you judge the marriage impossible?"

"Impossible, no; nothing is impossible. But... but I suppose that her will is indispensable, as indispensable as it is doubtful."

"Doubtful! Are you certain of that?"

Jorge had risen and taken a few steps, not wholly agitated, but a little out of his usual impassivity. The idea of marriage appeared to him now a little more possible and feasible, since his aunt had frankly proposed an alliance to him.

"Are you certain of that?" the baroness repeated.

"Certain, no; but there is every reason for doubt. Guiomar knows that I am fond of her; and yet she gives me not the slightest sign of returning my feelings."

Jorge set out at length all the reasons he had for believing that Guiomar's will did not correspond to his; he related to her, with the greatest exactness and fidelity, some three or four episodes that seemed to him good proof of what he said. The baroness did not hear it all with equal attention. When he finished:

"Guiomar is very easily vexed," she said, "and sometimes, for that very reason, has those cold appearances. Nothing prevents her, however, from coming to love you, if she does not already. There is in her a certain natural haughtiness, which may also explain that coldness; it seems to me it would be painful for her to receive the love of someone who thought to raise her up to himself."

"That, perhaps..."

"But that feeling, which may be and is honorable, is certainly not invincible."

All these words of the baroness flattered her nephew, on whose lips there now hovered a smile of inward satisfaction. From time to time he heard nothing of what his aunt said to him; his ears turned inward; he listened to himself. Guiomar's love began to seem possible to him; everything the baroness told him was reasonable, with the advantage of clearing up the obscure sides of the situation. Besides, how far did the baroness conjecture, or reveal? It might well be that she had read deeper into the girl's heart.

Jorge made these reflections while the baroness went on speaking and developing the idea she had lately indicated. Until that day he had limited all his action to a few glances and rare words of compliment; the interview with his aunt had given him animation; it seemed to him that the occasion had come to emerge from that armed peace.

Guiomar arrived shortly after and found them in the sewing room, an elegant euphemism that meant, literally, a little parlor for conversation interspersed with crochet. Mrs. Oswald came with her; both were laughing merrily at some episode seen on the road. Jorge had risen, slow but smiling, and pressed Guiomar's hand, pressed it in earnest, more than was usual and courteous. Guiomar did not seem troubled; she asked after his health, conveyed to her godmother the regards that had been sent her, and prepared to go out.

During that time Jorge looked at her, truly enraptured in the contemplation of all that noble figure, now more beautiful than before, since the long-dreamed alliance had become possible to him. There were in Jorge's eyes some lewd traces or other, from which one could gather that, if he had been a poet, and an Arcadian poet, he would have issued for the millionth time the comparison of Venus and her infallible little Cupids; a detestable comparison, above all because the chaste beauty of the girl, if anything pagan could be said of it, would rather be a Diana converted to the Gospel.

Jorge left there singularly agitated; the baroness's talk had given him nerve and resolution, and the picture of the marriage began to take shape in his mind, like the watch a child is to wear for the first time. Until then he had let himself drift with the waters; now he saw the necessity and the possibility of making for the happy shore of matrimony.

Jorge's doubts did not assail his mind; as soon as he reached home he seized his pen and set down on the white, glossy sheet of his paper an elegant and polished confession, which he nevertheless recast two or three times before he gave it as ready. The final draft finished, he copied that prose of the heart onto the cleanest sheet he had in the house, folded it, and put it in his pocket.

In the evening he went to his aunt's house. He found the ladies around a table; Guiomar was reading, for her godmother to hear, a French novel recently published in Paris and brought by the last packet. Mrs. Oswald was reading too, but to herself, a thick volume of Sir Walter Scott, the Constable edition, of Edinburgh.

Jorge came to interrupt them a little, but only to interrupt, for the reading continued soon after, he himself helping Guiomar in that filial task. The tea came, then the hour for retiring, and the baroness declared the evening over, although the book was nearly finished.

"One more chapter," ventured Jorge, with the book open in his hands.

The baroness smiled and turned her eyes to Guiomar, to whose account she put that devotion of her nephew's; she refused nonetheless, being ready to fall asleep.

"I, for one, will not go to bed without knowing the rest," declared Guiomar; "I am taking the book with me."

"Ah!" said Jorge with a gesture of satisfaction.

And while Guiomar prepared to accompany her godmother to the door of the bedroom, and Mrs. Oswald marked her page and closed her book, Jorge likewise closed the other, but with such delay and care that it gave the Englishwoman much to ponder. Whether she came to understand it, we shall see later; what is certain is that the book was finally handed to Guiomar, with the page marked, not with the ribbon that hung there, but with a little scrap of paper.

The little scrap of paper was the letter; only a few centimeters in height; but however meager its dimensions, it might well carry inside it nothing less than an approaching storm.
