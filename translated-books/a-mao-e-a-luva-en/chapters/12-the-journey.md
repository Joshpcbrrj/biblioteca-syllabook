# 12. The Journey

Scarcely had Luís Alves resumed his reading of the case papers when the servant came into the study and handed him a visiting card.

"Let him come in!" said the lawyer, reading the name of the baroness's nephew.

And soon the measured, slow step of the young man was heard in the corridor, and presently he appeared at the door of the study, making a bow, solemn but graceful.

"Am I disturbing you, doctor?" asked Jorge.

"For heaven's sake!" exclaimed the lawyer, rising and going to meet him at the door. "You could not disturb me in any case; least of all now, when the reading of some papers has fatigued me beyond measure, and the greatest fortune I could wish for is the presence of a man of wit."

Jorge thanked him for this somewhat emphatic compliment, and returned it with another flattery, much longer and of greater reach. Which is to say that he had come to ask for something. Indeed, after the minutes of preamble and the unreeling of generalities, Jorge drew himself up more stiffly than he had been till then and let fly this abrupt question:

"Do you know that I have come to ask you for something grave?"

Luís Alves bowed.

"Grave and simple at the same time," the baroness's nephew went on; "but before that I needed to know whether you are as much a friend of our family as our family is of you."

"Oh! certainly!"

"You are perhaps the least assiduous of the people who go there, in spite of being a neighbor; only now do I see you there more often; yet you are like a flower that gives itself away by its fragrance; my aunt has the best opinion in the world of you; she finds in you a gravity, and I feel it too, and I cannot even understand how a man could be anything else. Those frivolous minds..."

"Are insufferable," concluded Luís Alves, anxious to get to the object of the visit.

The object was the baroness's journey. A commander, a friend of the late baron and a planter in Cantagalo, had had a promise from the widow, two years before, that she would go and spend some time there. The baroness had always evaded keeping her word; now, however, such had been the insistence that she had resolved to go. Now what Jorge had come to propose was, in his own words, a conspiracy of friends to dissuade his aunt from that project. He assured the lawyer that, even if the conspiracy were discovered, his life would be safe and sound.

Luís Alves supposed at first that this was a mere pretext; but, having observed that the fair Guiomar was not indifferent to the young man, he understood that the latter had in the proposed conspiracy an entirely personal interest. At last Jorge went so far as to confess that if his aunt insisted on leaving the Court, he had no remedy but to accompany her.

The agreement was not difficult; it was settled that they would make every effort to dissuade the baroness. Jorge wished to leave at once; Luís Alves detained him a little longer, with expressions of praise skillfully woven and still more skillfully set into the conversation, and also letting himself go along with the other's turn of mind, accepting his ideas and his prejudices and applauding them discreetly, serious when they were or seemed to be serious, jocular when they came with an air of wit, answering, in short, every gesture and movement of the other, as the mirror does by office and obligation: the whole art, in short, of dealing with men, of attracting them and courting them, which he had learned early and which was to profit him later in public life.

That night Luís Alves went to the baroness's house, where there were few people, all of them intimates. The lady of the house, seated in her usual armchair, had beside her a lady of her own age, equally a widow, and opposite, the white and retired whiskers of a former civil servant. On a sofa, Mrs. Oswald and Jorge were to be seen talking, now in very low voices, now a little louder. Farther along, two young men were telling two ladies the plot of the latest play at the Ginásio. Farther still, a girl from the neighborhood was praising to another the scissors of Mme. Bragaldi, which, she said, challenged the brush of the scene painter, her husband. Finally, by one of the windows, a lively, pretty young girl was to be seen saying a thousand graceful trifles to another person, who was none other than our acquaintance Guiomar. The conversation, thus divided, became general at times, only to fall back at once into its former particularity; the groups also shifted from time to time, as did the subject, and so the hours were pleasantly killed, though they, poor things, neither resisted nor quickened their pace by so much as a minute.

Luís Alves had joined the baroness's group, which Jorge was not long in joining. The lawyer had the discretion to wait for the subject to come of itself, if it came, or to introduce it into the conversation when it seemed suitable. But Jorge, who was impatient, dragged the subject into debate. Luís Alves proved faithful to his word; he declared amiably that he opposed the journey, as a neighbor and friend, that he would in the last resort call for the aid of the public force; that it was an error and a crime to leave that house widowed of benevolence and grace and taste and all the other excellent qualities which the fortunate who frequented it found there; that, in short, the harm was so great that it could not fail to be a sin, though it was not listed in the catechisms, and as a sin it would surely be punished with bitter penalties in the next world; wherefore, and the rest of the case notwithstanding, it was his decision that the baroness should stay.

All these arguments were stated as they ought to be, in a gallant and jovial manner, to which the baroness answered in kind, and which would have yielded nothing more, had not Luís Alves, changing his style, put the matter on different ground.

"Let us tell the truth, Senhora Baroness; the journey is bound to be immensely uncomfortable for you, if that were all; your strength is surely not what it was in your first years; your health is delicate and will not bear so much fatigue. I confess that I speak in the name of a certain personal interest as friend and neighbor; but that is not the principal reason. If there were an urgent motive, well and good; but since it is merely a promise made so long ago, it would be cruelty on my part not to insist that you stay."

The baroness defended herself, and Luís Alves was not slow to recognize, in his own mind, that she did not defend herself with the vigor of an original and personal resolution. The conversation, meanwhile, had become more general; from all sides came votes of opposition.

For some minutes Guiomar had not been attending to the person she was talking with; she had her ear sharpened and trained upon her godmother's group. No one was observing her; but it is the privilege of the novelist and the reader to see in a character's face what the others do not see or cannot see. In Guiomar's face we can read not only the boredom that unanimous opinion against the baroness's project caused her, but also the expression of an imperious and willful temper.

"We are agreed, I believe?" Luís Alves asked, looking alternately at the baroness and the other people.

"It is not possible, doctor," answered the good lady.

"Certainly it is not possible," Guiomar put in from where she sat. "The journey offers no risk, nor is my godmother an invalid. Besides, it is a promise made; it cannot be left unkept."

This opinion, spoken in a dry and firm tone, though the voice lost nothing of its natural velvet, amounted to a little cold water thrown upon the triumphant boiling of spirits.

"Guiomar is right," said the baroness; "now it is necessary to go; it is only three or four months."

Luís Alves looked long at Guiomar, as if seeking to see in her face all the antecedents of the baroness's resolution. The opposition slackened; Jorge called in vain upon the lawyer for his help. His aunt's resolution, if ever it had been shaken, became firm again.

Guiomar, meanwhile, had risen and come over to her godmother's group. Jorge gazed at her with an expression of vanity and covetousness. Luís Alves, who was standing, stepped back a little to let her pass. The eyes with which he contemplated her were neither of covetousness nor of vanity; the lady reader, who will still remember the confession he himself made to Estêvão, will perhaps suppose that they were of love. Perhaps, who knows? a somewhat tranquil love, not mad and blind like Estêvão's, not puerile and lascivious like Jorge's, a middle term between the one and the other, such as there could be in the heart of an ambitious man.

"Dr. Luís Alves defends bad causes," said Guiomar, smiling at him; "it is not a question of something impossible. As for me, Cantagalo has only one drawback; it will be less amusing than the Court; but time passes quickly..."

"In that case," said Jorge, sighing, "I too shall forgo theaters and balls; I sacrifice myself to the family."

"Do you want to come with us?" asked the baroness cheerfully.

"Of course!"

Guiomar bit her lower lip, with an expression of spite, which she was able to contain and stifle without anyone's perceiving it, no one, except Luís Alves. A tranquil and perspicacious smile brushed the lawyer's lips, while the girl, to hide the impression that remained with her, turned again to the window, where she stayed some moments alone, half turned outward and half sheltered by the shadow the curtain made there. A sound of steps made her turn back inward. It was Luís Alves.

"Ah!" she said, feigning tranquillity, "I thank you for not having insisted further on your counsels."

"The intention was good," answered Luís Alves in a low voice; "but now it will be excellent; not all is lost: I undertake to save the rest."

Guiomar frowned with the liveliest and most natural astonishment; such astonishment that it seemed to have made her forget another, equally natural feeling: that of the spite which that singular familiarity would cause her. But amazement overcame everything; Guiomar felt that he had read in her the reason for her insistence and the displeasure at the result.

The frown vanished little by little, but the girl did not at once withdraw her eyes. There was in them an imperious question, which the soul did not dare to convey to the lips. If there is some question on the reader's lips, let us wait for the next chapter.
