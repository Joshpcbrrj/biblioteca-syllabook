# 18. The School

Mrs. Oswald had spoken too much. The baroness had not charged her with telling the goddaughter the reason she was being sent for. But that indiscretion was not the only one. Mrs. Oswald, instead of slipping away and leaving the matter that was to bring Guiomar and the baroness together to be dealt with between them, yielded to curiosity and accompanied the girl.

The baroness was seated between two windows, with the letter open in her hands, so intent on rereading it that she did not hear the sound of Guiomar's and Mrs. Oswald's feet.

"Did you call me, godmother?" asked Guiomar, stopping in front of her.

The baroness raised her head.

"Ah! Yes, it is true; I called you. Sit down here."

Guiomar drew up the nearest chair and sat down beside the baroness. The latter had slowly folded the letter, and had her eyes on the floor, as if looking for where she should begin. When she raised them she came upon the Englishwoman. She was about to speak, but stopped short. The affection she had for her did not prevent her from finding Mrs. Oswald's presence on such an occasion too familiar. She waited a few moments; but as the Englishwoman seemed entirely absent-minded:

"Mrs. Oswald," said the baroness, "go and see whether the little birds have been fed yet."

The Englishwoman perceived that these little birds, in this case, were a pure metaphor, and that the baroness was doing no more than delicately asking her to go away. Nevertheless, she did not take the hint.

"I think not," she said; "I shall find out at once."

"Look," said the baroness when she was already halfway across, "pull those doors to, and give orders that no one interrupt us."

The Englishwoman obeyed and went out. The grimace she made on leaving no one could see, and nothing was lost.

The two were left alone.

"Sit here, Guiomar," said the baroness, pointing to a little stool at her feet.

Guiomar left the chair and went to sit on the stool, lovingly resting her arms on her godmother's knees. The latter clasped her head in her hands, and so remained a long time without speaking, but eloquent in that silence, in which the word belonged to the heart. Both were moved; and Guiomar, together with a sigh, murmured this single, sweet name:

"Mamma!"

It was the first time she had given her that name, and so deeply did it sink into the baroness's soul that her answer was to cover her with kisses.

"Yes, your mother," said her godmother; "she who gave you life would not love you more than I. You have the soul and the tenderness of the daughter heaven took from me, and if all mothers who lose children could replace them in the same way, the greatest and most cruel pain that exists in the world would vanish from it..."

Guiomar's answer was to press her hands and kiss them. A pause followed, in which the emotion little by little disappeared, and the baroness looked at Luís Alves's letter, crumpled by Guiomar's gesture.

"Guiomar," she said at last, "have you thought about last night's request?"

The girl had expected her godmother to speak to her of Luís Alves's request; the baroness's question disconcerted her a little. Her intelligence, however, was clear and shrewd; her answer was another question:

"Is one night enough to decide the whole rest of one's life?" she said, smiling.

"You are right, my child; but the question was natural from one who wishes to see a desire fulfilled. Jorge asked for your hand. Do you know that he has an excellent character?"

"Excellent," answered the girl.

"A good soul," the baroness went on, "and a distinguished young man. He seems to be very fond of you, from what he said last night, does he not? It is natural; I am only surprised that many more do not love you."

The baroness stopped; Guiomar played with the fringes of her sleeve, not daring to raise her eyes.

"You must know," the baroness continued, "that I would be glad to see this marriage take place; I am convinced that it would make you happy, and him too, at least as far as one can judge of present things... What does your heart say?"

And as Guiomar did not answer at once:

"Ah! I forgot what you told me a moment ago. One night is not enough to decide the whole rest of one's life. Well; you will hear two more things from me. The first is that... Read this letter yourself."

The baroness gave the letter to Guiomar, who opened it and read Luís Alves's request for her hand. While she ran her eyes over the few written lines, her godmother seemed to observe her fixedly, as if trying to read in her face the impression the request made on her, whether surprise or satisfaction. There was no apparent surprise or satisfaction; Guiomar read the letter and handed it back to her godmother.

"Have you read it? That is the first thing I wanted to tell you. Dr. Luís Alves asks you in marriage; you have to choose between him and Jorge. The second thing is that of the two suitors Jorge is the one my heart prefers; but it is not I who am marrying, it is you; choose with full freedom the one who speaks to your heart."

Guiomar drew herself up and looked directly at her godmother, with such signs of astonishment on her face that the latter could not help asking her:

"What is the matter?"

The girl did not answer; I mean she did not answer her with her lips; she seized her hand and pressed it between her own, and stayed looking at her as if reflecting. The expression of her face had passed from astonishment to satisfaction and from this to something that seemed at once indignation and disgust.

"Oh! godmother!" exclaimed Guiomar, "why did our hearts not understand each other at once? There was no need to put between them an importunate and disheartening spirit. If I had guessed the words you have just spoken, I would not have suffered half of what they have made me suffer for these many days..."

"Suffer?"

"Suffer; nothing less. But let us leave that. It was your heart that spoke and mine that listened; I can now tell you frankly what I feel, without fear of afflicting you."

She had no need to say more; the choice she was about to make was already indicated, at least. The baroness understood it, and her face darkened and she sighed. Her goddaughter heard the sigh and perceived the sudden sadness; she repented of having gone so far.

"I understand," answered the baroness; "you mean that of the two suitors you choose Dr. Luís Alves?"

The girl remained silent; her godmother looked at her with an expression of anxiety that distressed her.

"Speak," the baroness repeated.

"I choose... Mr. Jorge," sighed Guiomar after a few moments.

The baroness started.

"Are you serious? I do not believe it; that is not the feeling of your heart. One can see that it is not. You wish to deceive me, and yourself too. I understand that you do not love him; you never loved him. But you love the other, do you not? What of it? It does not give me the pleasure I should have had if... What does it matter, if you are happy? Your happiness is above my preferences. It was a dream of mine; I desired it with all my strength; I would have done what I could to attain it; but one does not force the heart, a heart above all like yours! You choose the other? Then you shall marry him."

The reader sees that the expected word, the word the girl felt coming from her heart to her lips and wanting to burst them, was not pronounced by her but by her godmother; and if the reader has attended to what precedes, he will see that this was exactly what she desired. But why had Jorge's name brushed her lips? The girl did not wish to deceive the baroness, but to translate unfaithfully the voice of her heart, so that her godmother might herself compare the translation with the original. There was in this something of an indirect means, of tactics, of affectation, I am almost saying of hypocrisy, if the word were not taken amiss. There was; but this very thing tells them that this Guiomar, without losing the excellences of her heart, was of the common clay of which God made our not very sincere humanity; and it will also tell them that, despite her green years, she already understood that the appearance of a sacrifice is often worth more than the sacrifice itself.

The baroness had finished speaking. The joy on Guiomar's face confirmed her first impression, and if the choice was contrary to what she desired, the satisfaction of her goddaughter repaid her for all she was about to lose. Such was that mother's soul: good, devoted and generous.

"Oh! godmother! thank you!" exclaimed the girl. "You do not hate me?"

"Oh!" exclaimed the baroness in a tone of reproach.

And she drew her to her and embraced her lovingly. Guiomar returned the movement, and the two mingled their inner joys and sincere affections.

Mrs. Oswald saw them a little later, smiling and in accord. It was easy to conclude which of the two suitors had won; Guiomar would not have received the baroness's nephew with so good a countenance. All was over; and perhaps her own person had suffered in that last stroke. The baroness had asked Guiomar to explain to what sufferings she had alluded, but the girl preferred to say nothing, not only so as not to afflict her godmother, but also so as not to give an aspect of rivalry to the situation between herself and Mrs. Oswald.

The choice was made, the consent given. The baroness answered the fortunate suitor that same afternoon. Estêvão would have noisily displayed all the joy such an answer caused him; his passionate and exuberant soul would have told God and men of that immense fortune. Luís Alves shut his pleasure, great as it was, within himself; he thought of the girl and of the future for a few moments, but spoke of them to no one.

The baroness wrote that same day to her nephew, communicating Guiomar's answer. Readers will have no difficulty in admitting that Jorge's heart did not feel the blow profoundly, but it felt something. He did not go to his aunt's house that night; nor on the second; on the third he got as far as going down the stairs; on the fourth he headed for Botafogo.

"All is over," his aunt said to him, truly grieved.

"Over!" sighed Jorge.

"Now you must have courage; I hope you will be a man."

"Oh! I shall be a man!" sighed Jorge again.

And two sighs torn from the breast of so grave a man must needs be two very grave sighs, as the reader easily believes.

In fact the young man's countenance showed neither dejection nor affliction; not the slightest trace of an ill-slept night crumpled it, still less of dried tears. Cheerful it was not, but grave and austere, as he always wore it, in contrast with the stiffness of his mustache.

The baroness nevertheless imagined that her nephew's grief must have mortified him greatly; she pressed his hands tenderly and said a few more words of encouragement.

Imagine what Jorge's first meeting with Guiomar would be. The girl was serene, perhaps smiling and even compassionate. Had she had to marry him she would surely have hated him; now she already forgave him his love. Jorge, for his part, could not help being somewhat shaken, partly emotion, partly constraint, the constraint being greater than the emotion. On his lips there hovered one of those smiles in which the penetrating eye of the people, or their picturesque imagination, has discovered the color yellow. Had his aspect been different, it is probable that she would have kept at least her respect for him. But that smile lost him entirely in Guiomar's mind.

At the first opportunity that offered, Jorge unburdened himself to Mrs. Oswald.

"Everything is lost..." he murmured.

The Englishwoman did not answer.

Jorge went on speaking, and the Englishwoman went on listening, but only listening, and wishing to divert him from the subject.

"Everything is lost," said the baroness's nephew at last, "perhaps through your fault."

"Mine?" asked Mrs. Oswald.

"Yours."

"But..."

Jorge hesitated a moment.

"You did not show enough warmth," he said at last.

"What would you have?" said Mrs. Oswald. "The heart cannot be mastered, nor is there any way to impose a feeling on it. Dona Guiomar is a saintly creature, she truly loves her rival; is there anything more just than to marry them?"

"So that..."

"So that everything was permissible on the supposition that she loved no other, but once she does..."

Luís Alves, on the evening of the day he received the letter, went to the baroness's house, and she received him with the best of her smiles. Guiomar's happiness made her completely happy; no wrath, no resentment, as Mrs. Oswald had announced. The whole house of cards had fallen to the ground, once the baroness's sincerity intervened.
