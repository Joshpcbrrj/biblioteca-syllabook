# 1. The End of the Letter

"But what do you mean to do now?"

"Die."

"Die? What an idea! Give it up, Estêvão. No one dies over so little..."

"One does. Anyone who has not suffered these pains cannot judge them. The blow went deep, and my heart is faint; however hateful the idea of death may seem, living is worse, far worse. Ah! you do not know what this is?"

"I do: a failed courtship..."

"Luís!"

"...And if a man died in every case of a failed courtship, the human race would already be much diminished, and Malthus would have wasted his breath. Come, come up."

Estêvão thrust his hand into his hair with a gesture of anguish; Luís Alves shook his head and smiled. The two stood in the hallway of Luís Alves's house, on Rua da Constituição, which was then called Rua dos Ciganos; then, that is, in 1853, a trifle of twenty years gone by, taking with them, perhaps, the reader's illusions, and leaving in exchange (the usurers!) a sad, raw and cheerless experience.

It was nine o'clock at night; Luís Alves was coming home at the very moment Estêvão was on his way to look for him; they met at the door. Right there Estêvão confided to him everything that had happened, which the reader will learn shortly, if these love stories, old as Adam and eternal as the heavens, do not bore them. The two friends lingered a while longer in the hallway, the one insisting that the other come upstairs, the other stubbornly saying he wanted to go and die, both so tenacious that there would have been no way to overcome them, had a compromise not occurred to Luís.

"Very well," he said, "I agree that you must die, but it shall be tomorrow. Give way on your part, and come spend the night with me. In these last hours you have to live on earth, you will give me a lesson in love, which I will repay with another in philosophy."

So saying, Luís Alves took Estêvão by the arm, and this time Estêvão did not resist, whether because the idea of death had not truly taken root in his brain, or because he yielded to the painful pleasure of speaking of the woman he loved, or, what is most likely, for both these reasons together. Let us go with them, up the stairs, as far as the drawing room, where Luís went to kiss his mother's hand.

"Mama," he said, "you will do me the favor of sending tea up to my room; Estêvão is spending the night with me."

Estêvão murmured a few words, which he tried to give an air of pleasantry, but which were as funereal as a cypress. By the light of the stearin candles Luís then saw a redness in his eyes, and guessed, which was not hard, that he had been weeping. Poor fellow! he sighed inwardly. From there the two went to the bedroom, which was a vast room with three beds, chairs of every shape, two bookcases and a desk, serving at once as alcove and study.

The tea came up shortly. At the urging of his host, Estêvão drank two sips; he lit a cigarette and began to pace the length of the room, while Luís Alves, preferring a cigar and a sofa, lit the former and stretched out on the latter, folding his hands blissfully over his belly and contemplating the tips of his slippers, with the placidity of a man none of whose courtships has failed. The silence was not complete; one could hear the rumble of carriages passing outside; in the room, however, the only sound was Estêvão's boots on the cane matting of the floor.

These two young men attended the academy at São Paulo, Luís Alves being in his fourth year and Estêvão in his third. They had met at the academy and become intimate friends, as far as two different natures could be, or perhaps for the very reason that they were different. Estêvão, endowed with extreme sensitivity and no less weakness of spirit, affectionate and good, not with that manly goodness which is the prerogative of a strong soul, but with that other goodness, soft and waxen, which yields to every circumstance, had, besides all this, the misfortune of still wearing on his nose the rose-colored spectacles of his virginal illusions. Luís Alves saw well with the eyes in his head. He was not a bad fellow, but he had his grain of selfishness, and if he was not incapable of affection, he knew how to govern it, moderate it, and above all guide it toward his own interest. Between these two men an intimate friendship had been formed, born in one from sympathy, in the other from habit. They were each other's natural confidants, with the difference that Luís Alves gave less than he received, and even so, not everything he gave expressed great trust.

Estêvão had long since related to his friend the whole story of the love, now come to nothing, its hopes, discouragements and glories, and, finally, its unexpected outcome. The poor fellow, who had been leafing through the most delightful chapter of the romance, in his own opinion, had fallen from the full height of his illusions into the harshest, most prosaic and wretched reality.

Estêvão's sweetheart (it is time to say something of her) was a girl of seventeen, and, for the moment, a mere pupil-teacher at the school kept by an aunt of our student, on Rua dos Inválidos. Estêvão had first seen her six months before, and at once felt himself bound to her "until death," as he told his friend when relating the meeting, which made him smile at so lengthy a term. Whatever the fatal term of that captivity might be, the truth is that Estêvão loved her the very moment he saw her, as one loves for the first time in one's life, a love somewhat headlong and blind, but sincere and pure. Did she love him? Estêvão said so, and had to believe it; a few tender glances, half a dozen meaningful handclasps, though at long intervals, gave him to understand that the heart of Guiomar (for Guiomar was her name) was not deaf to the student's passion. But beyond that, nothing more, or little more.

The little more was a flower, not picked from the stem in all its original freshness, but already withered and scentless, and not given, but asked for.

"Will you do me a favor?" Estêvão said one day, pointing to the flower she wore in her hair. "That flower is withered, and you will naturally throw it away when you undo your hair; I wished you would give it to me."

Guiomar, smiling, took the flower from her hair and gave it to him; Estêvão received it with the same contentment he would have felt had his share of heaven been granted him in advance. Besides the flower, and to make up for the letters, of which there were none, Estêvão had obtained nothing else during those six long months, except for those glances, which are after all only glances, and go away with the eyes they came from. Was that love, caprice, pastime, or what else was it?

That afternoon, the fatal afternoon, when the two were alone, which was rare and difficult, he told her that he would soon be returning to São Paulo, carrying her image with him, and asked her in exchange to write to him at least once. Guiomar frowned and fixed on him her magnificent pair of brown eyes, with so much irritation and dignity that the poor young man was left astonished and perplexed.

One can imagine his anguish before the silence that reigned between them for a few seconds; what one cannot imagine is the grief that prostrated him, the grief and the amazement, when she, rising from the chair where she sat, answered him as she went out:

"Forget all about it."

"As for me," said Luís Alves, hearing for the third time the account of so cruel an ending, "as for me, I would obey her to the letter; I would forget it and go and cure myself over my textbooks; Roman Law and Philosophy, I know no better remedy for such ailments."

Estêvão did not hear his friend's words; he was then sitting on the bed, with his elbows planted on his knees and his head in his hands, seeming to weep. At first he wept in silence; but it was not long before Luís Alves saw him throw himself down on the bed, writhe convulsively, sobbing, stifling as best he could the cries that came from his chest, pulling his hair, begging for death, all of it mingled with the name of Guiomar, so heartfelt, so pitifully natural, that at last it moved him, and there was nothing for it but to say a few words of comfort. The consolation came in time; the grief, having reached its paroxysm, declined little by little, and the tears dried, at least for a while.

"I know all this must seem ridiculous to you," said Estêvão, sitting up on the bed, "but what would you have? I lived in the persuasion that I was loved, and perhaps I was. For that very reason I do not understand what happened today. Either what I took to be love was perhaps no more than a pastime or a mockery..."

"Perhaps, perhaps," Luís Alves interrupted, understanding that the best way to cure him of love was to prick his pride.

Estêvão remained pensive for a few moments.

"No, it is not possible," he objected. "You do not know her. She is a grave and noble creature, incapable of conceiving a feeling like that, which would be vulgar or cruel."

"Women..."

"I have wondered whether today's scene was not a way of testing me, of seeing how far I loved her... You need not laugh, Luís; I affirm nothing; I say it may be so. It is no wonder if she made that calculation, a good calculation, in that case, wholly born of the heart..."

Estêvão's imagination went down this slope of flowery conjectures, and Luís Alves judged it wise not to frighten his horses. Off it went, and on, down that way, reins slack and a laugh on its lips. Bon voyage! his colleague exclaimed inwardly, stretching out again on the sofa. The journey was not long, but it had a salutary effect on the lover's spirits, sweetening his sorrows, a circumstance Luís Alves took advantage of to speak to him of a hundred things foreign to the heart and to divert him from the thought that absorbed him. He succeeded in his aim for half an hour, and succeeded in more, for he made his colleague laugh, at first with a bitter and doubtful laugh, then with a jovial and frank one, incompatible with tragic designs. But alas! his grief was a kind of moral cough, which abated and returned, sometimes intense, sometimes weaker, but always unfailing. The boy chanced to open a page of Werther; he read half a dozen lines, and the fit returned stronger than ever.

Luís Alves came to his aid with the lozenges of consolation; the fit passed; new conversation, new laughter, new despair, and so the hours of the night slipped away, which the clock in the dining room struck dryly and regularly, as if to remind the two friends that our passions neither hasten nor slow the pace of time.

For the two students dawn coincided with the strokes of noon, which is no wonder, since they fell asleep only when it was beginning to put out the stars. Estêvão passed the night (the morning, I mean) quite peacefully and free of bad dreams. When he opened his eyes he found the room and the objects around him strange. As soon as he recognized them, his heart awoke along with his memory, and in it there was no longer the sharp pain of the evening before. The events, though recent, were beginning to wrap themselves in the twilight shadow of the past.

Nature has her imperious laws; and man, a complex being, lives not only on what he loves, but also (we are bound to say it) on what he eats. Let this serve as an excuse for our student, who breakfasted that day as on the days before, it being enough to say in his favor that if he did not do so with tears, neither did he do so gladly. But it is certain that the tempest had calmed; what remained was an undertow, still strong, but one that would diminish with time. Luís Alves avoided speaking to him of Guiomar; Estêvão was the first to recall her.

"Give time time," answered Luís Alves, "and you will yet laugh at your plans of yesterday. Above all, thank fate that you escaped so quickly. And do you want some advice?"

"Say it."

"Love is a letter, more or less long, written on vellum paper, gilt-edged, highly scented and spruce; a letter of congratulation when you read it, a letter of condolence when you have finished reading it. You who have reached the end, put the epistle at the bottom of a drawer, and do not think of going to see whether it has a 'postscript'..."

Estêvão applauded the metaphor with a smile of good omen.

Twice he saw the lovely Guiomar before leaving for São Paulo. The first time he still felt shaken, because the wound had not wholly healed; the second, he was able to face her without disturbance. It would have been better, or more romantic at least, had I sent him on his way to the academy with despair in his heart, bathed in tears, or drinking them in silence, as his dignity as a man demanded. But what can I do about it? He left here with dry eyes, amusing himself against the tedium of the journey with some young man's jest, a young man once more, as before.
