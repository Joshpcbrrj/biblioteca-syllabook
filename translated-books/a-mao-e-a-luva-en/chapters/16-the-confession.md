# 16. The Confession

On the same night when Jorge, yielding to Mrs. Oswald's suggestions, was attempting the last resort that in the Englishwoman's opinion remained, Luís Alves was at home, comfortably seated in a leather armchair before the window, his eyes on the sea and his thoughts on his two victorious candidacies. Midnight was just striking when someone stepped down from a tilbury and knocked at his door.

It was Estêvão.

Luís Alves was naturally surprised to see him there at that hour; but Estêvão explained everything.

"I have come to spend half an hour with you, or the whole night if you wish. I was at home, bored, thinking... you know very well of what..."

"Of her?" Luís Alves interrupted.

"Now and always."

Luís Alves twisted his mustache and looked three or four times at his colleague, who was taking off his hat and preparing to fetch a chair and sit beside the other.

"Estêvão," said Luís Alves after a few moments of reflection, turning the armchair around to face the room, "hear me first, and then you will decide whether to stay the night or leave at once. Perhaps you will choose the latter course."

"Are you going to speak to me of Guiomar?"

"Precisely."

Estêvão sat down facing Luís Alves. His heart beat fast; one would have said his whole life hung from his friend's lips. There was a moment of silence.

"No... no hope, then?" murmured Estêvão.

"You have said the fatal word!" exclaimed Luís Alves. "Yes, you have no hope."

"But... how do you know?"

"Do not question me; I could not tell you all there is. Spare me, at least, that sad duty."

Estêvão felt his eyes fill with tears. He tried to speak, but the words came out wrapped in sobs.

Luís Alves smoked tranquilly, following with his eyes the little rings of smoke that escaped from the tip of his cigar. This silence lasted about ten minutes. The sea beat evenly on the shore. The voice of the wave and the barking of a dog in the distance were the only sounds that came to break the stillness of that hour, solemn for one of those two men, who was about to lose even the repose of hope.

Estêvão was the first to speak:

"She loves another, does she not?" he asked, in a trembling voice.

"She does," answered Luís Alves, in a dull tone.

Estêvão rose and took a few steps about the room without a word, biting the tip of his mustache, pausing at times, at others expressing with a disordered gesture the feelings that tumbled in his heart. The pain must have been great, but its manifestation was no longer the same as the reader saw two years before, when he went to confide to his friend Guiomar's first disappointment.

"It seems to me I guessed this very thing," he said at last, stopping in front of Luís Alves. "This desire that came over me to come here, at this hour, not certain of finding you, was one more kindness of my destiny. I ought to have expected it. What a life mine has been, Luís! I clung, I know not why, to the hope of being loved by her, of winning her by pity, or by remorse, or by whatever other motive it might be; the motive mattered little... The essential thing was that she should repay me in tenderness and love for all the pains I have suffered, all the tears I have swallowed in silence... And it was that hope alone that still gave me strength... that made me believe myself happy, as a wretch can be, as I could be, who was born under an evil star... Oh! if you knew... No, you do not know, nor does she, nobody knows or will ever know all that I have suffered, all that..."

He broke off. Two tears, squeezed from the bottom of his heart, leapt from his eyes and ran swiftly down to lose themselves among the sparse, fine hairs of his beard. He felt that others might follow, and went to sit on a sofa, half turned away from Luís Alves. The others came, for the heart still had them for supreme sorrows; but they ran silently, without a sob, without a single complaint.

Luís Alves had risen and gone to the window. His spirit, though cold and quiet, seemed now a little stirred. It was not pain; and I do not know whether it could be called remorse. Mere unease, and compassion. His heart was capable of affections; but, as was said in the first chapter, he knew how to rule them, moderate them and guide them to his own interest. He was neither corrupt nor wicked; neither can it be said that he was devoted or chivalrous; he was, at bottom, a coldly ambitious man.

Estêvão had risen again and taken up his hat.

"Come here," said Luís Alves, coming in and going up to him; "I see that you are more of a man than before. It remains for you to be one completely; sweep from your memory and your heart everything that might refer..."

"What remedy is there!" interrupted Estêvão, smiling bitterly; "what remedy have I but to forget her! But when?"

"Sooner perhaps than you suppose..."

Luís Alves did not finish; Estêvão had looked at him with a gesture of astonishment and had gone to sit down again.

"Sooner than I suppose!" he exclaimed. "You have no heart: you have not even observation or memory. Do you not see, do you not feel that this passion is the blood of my blood, the life of my life? To forget her! It would be well if I could forget her; but my evil fate tears even that hope from me, because this inner, constant suffering will go with me to my death..."

This time it was Luís Alves who paced from side to side. An idea was dawning in his mind, and he was examining it, to see whether to put it into execution then and there. It was to tell him everything. Estêvão would learn it later; better that he should learn it at once and from him. At the same time he reflected on the exaltation of the young man's feelings; his pain would certainly be aggravated on learning that it was he whom Guiomar preferred. The heart, which would forgive a stranger, would condemn a friend.

Estêvão, seated, with his eyes on the ceiling, seemed given over to his reflections, but only seemed so, for he was not thinking; he was evoking old memories, making Guiomar's gentle figure rise before his eyes, feeling the sway of her lovely brown eyes, hearing her sweet, velvety speech pour itself into his heart. He did not merely evoke, he also created, painting with his imagination the happiness the girl could give him if she had chosen him among all men, if the two of them bound their destinies together. He saw her at his side, he put his arm around her waist, he filled her hair with kisses, all this in the midst of a landscape unique on earth, because the abundance of nature would grow at the touch of that pure, chaste and eternal feeling. I do not speak, reader; I merely transcribe, faithfully, the lover's imaginings; I fix upon this sheet of paper the flights he took through that space, the only bliss that was permitted him.

Luís Alves woke him from these visions.

"You have reason to feel," he said; "but do not wear out your heart, for there are greater surprises in life... In any case, let me tell you that you have no reason for reproach..."

"Do I reproach anyone?"

"There is in love a germ of hatred that may develop later. Perhaps you will come to accuse her of not wanting you; on that day reflect that the movements of the heart are not in the hands of the will. She is not to blame if another has awakened her love."

"Ah! She has charged you with her defense!"

Luís Alves smiled; he had counted on the recrimination.

"No, she has not charged me with her defense," he said; "it is I who take it into my own hands. What do I defend here but nature, reason, the logic of the feelings, hard and inflexible like all other logic? There is at the bottom of your words a feeling of selfishness..."

"Love is nothing else," answered Estêvão, smiling in his turn. "Do you want me, on top of everything, to thank her for this despair? Do you want me to go and shake the hand of the man who knew how to win her?"

Luís Alves bit the tip of his lip and drew near the window. As he was about to turn back inside he heard a noise at the next window, the first of the baroness's house. Luís Alves took one step more.

He saw no one; he saw only the remnant of a dress fleeing and an object that fell at his feet.

He stooped to pick it up. It was a large sheet of paper wrapped, to give it more weight, around another, small one folded in four. Luís Alves went near the light and quickly read what was written there. He read it, put the paper in his pocket and went, with an air of unconcern, to the window.

No one; the baroness's house slept.

When he came back inside, Estêvão had risen. He had seen Luís Alves pick up the paper and read it. He understood nothing of what had happened; but his look seemed to ask for an explanation.

Luís Alves went straight to the point.

"Estêvão," he said, "you are going to learn the whole truth; I could not hide from you what has happened, nor would it perhaps be fitting for you to learn it from another's mouth. Guiomar might have loved you, you were worthy of her, and she of you; but nature did not make you for one another. They are two excellent souls who would be unhappy united. Who is there to blame here? But if nature explains her feeling, it equally explains that of a third person, who is myself. You confided to me the pains and hopes of your heart; it was to know all my friendship and the deep esteem I have always had for you. But neither you nor I counted on me; for I too have a heart, and the spell of beauty speaks also to my soul. I could not look on her coldly. Passion clouded me. In this my happiness of loving and being loved, believe that I am somewhat unhappy, because there are your tears, there is your long and cruel suffering, which I imagine and deplore. The confession is frank; I do not speak to you of repentance, because these are acts of the heart and not of the conscience, which is pure and honorable. And after this faithful account, I trust you will lament with me the meeting in which chance or ill luck brought the three of us together; but you will not accuse me, nor refuse me your old esteem. I speak only of esteem; friendship, I believe, cannot be the same. But you will respect my character. For my part, neither the one nor the other perishes; I know what you are worth. I do not know where the wave of destiny will cast us tomorrow. For the last time, however, I hope you will clasp your friend's hand."

Luís Alves ended by holding out his hand. Estêvão looked at him, but said not a word, made not a single gesture: he walked to the door and went out.

"Estêvão!" cried Luís Alves.

But only the sound of feet going down answered him, and a little later that of the tilbury rolling dully over the damp earth of the beach.

Luís Alves shrugged his shoulders curtly; he went up to the light and reread the writing.
